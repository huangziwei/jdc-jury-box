## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Nobody in this job should expect too much. Even when my luck brings more than one worthy item in a given month, I can’t hope for balance: that fair-play detective novels, say, will match an equal number of thrillers. But once or twice the miracle occurs, the scissors snip a rounded pattern, and for February (*mirabile dictu*!) two detective novels hold our scale-pans level against two superior adventures in espionage.

Though the sleuths are no better than the spies—you may even prefer cloak-and-dagger—let’s begin with detection because that has always been our custom.

Far from being instruction or advice, **Gently with the Ladies** by Alan Hunter (Macmillan, $5.95) refers to Chief Superintendent George Gently, ace investigator of Scotland Yard.

Clytie Fazakerly, wealthy wife of parasitic idler John Fazakerly, is battered to death in their luxury flat after a hundredth violent quarrel with her husband. Though few doubt this waster’s guilt, Superintendent Gently remains unconvinced.

Temperamental Clytie, short for Clytemnestra, may well have been slain by a woman. There’s arrogant Sybil Bannister, with whom she had a lesbian affair. Brenda Merryn, her half-sister and probable heir, has been John Fazakerly’s mistress. We hear much of Sarah Johnson, Fazakerly’s current mistress. And always in shadow lurks elusive Beryl Rogers, unseen almost to the end.

Gently, at heart something of a romantic, treads the maze amid conflicting evidence. He himself is tempted by the flesh; crisis succeeds crisis, including a near-suicide above rooftops, before Gently traps the murderer in this well-plotted mystery with every clue in sight.

A different kind of detective, a different background for very different action, pleasantly characterize Arthur D. Goldstein’s **You’re Never Too Old To Die** (Random House, $5.95).

Amiable, sharp-witted Max Guttman, seventy-two-year-old New York manufacturer, has retired to live with his daughter and her family in California. His days he spends at the Golden Valley Senior Citizens' Center, a peaceful haven where elderly ladies and gentlemen can foregather.

Sometimes peaceful, yes. Rich resident Samuel Cohen, falling in love with almost-as-old Miss Walters, dies of a heart attack as soon as they become engaged. When the lady swears her *fiancé* was murdered, nobody in authority will believe her. Whereupon Miss Walters dies, too.

Detecting against his will, Max Guttman sees light through a murk of emotional conflict. “Sherlock Holmes I ain’t,” Max has declared. Yet in true Baker Street tradition he interprets the design and snatches off somebody’s mask. Let’s hope we meet him again.

Michael Lord, protagonist of **The Man with Two Clocks** by Whit Masterson (Dodd, Mead, $5.95) must rank among the most sympathetic amateur agents ever blackmailed into espionage.

Young Lord, brilliant nuclear physicist, has invented a classic secret weapon called Airbrush. With university work he enjoys, with a wife he adores, Lord walks straight into nightmare. Ruthless Emlyn Shade, National Security, inexplicably prepares charges of treason.

A certain three-months’ vacation, actually spent in the woods with his old friend Ben Flagg, he is accused of spending elsewhere and in Russian hands, betraying Airbrush’s secret. Witnesses say they never heard of Ben Flagg, as well as other nonsense which makes Lord reel. He can clear his name, he learns, only by undertaking a dangerous secret mission to a Russian research station in the Antarctic.

Excitement mounts at that frozen waste. He matches wits with the KGB’s best agent, slippery Vasily Nikitin. He finds an answer to all perplexities. His triumphant return, climaxed by one final stunning surprise, rounds out the work of a master storyteller who belongs at the very top.

Derek Lambert’s **The Yermakov Transfer** (Dutton, $7.95) features a Russian plot to kidnap the head of the Soviet Union for the one cause most men regard as just.

Though I have left insufficient space, it may be said that Derek Lambert draws Russian characters as vividly as he draws the American newspaperman or the British blonde in this fast-paced drama of intrigue. Another A-1 choice for your list.
