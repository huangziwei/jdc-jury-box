For fact-crime connoisseurs the ghastly fate of Mr. and Mrs. Andrew J. Borden at Fall River, Mass., is studied at some length in Robert Sullivan’s **Goodbye Lizzie Borden** (Stephen Greene, $8.95).

At high noon of an August day, 1892, some malignant hand with a hatchet slaughtered wealthy Andrew J. and his second wife in the cramped little house they shared with Emma and Lizzie, the old man’s daughters by a previous marriage, and an Irish maidservant named Bridget.

Thirty-two-year-old Lizzie, the enigmatic daughter, was arrested, tried, and, as a well-brought-up New England girl of the Christian Endeavor, triumphantly acquitted. Inheriting half her father’s fortune, she bought a fine house in Fall River and lived there alone until her own death in 1927.

But did she do it, or didn’t she? Controversy still simmers. In **Goodbye Lizzie Borden**, by a distinguished jurist, you will find little speculation. The facts, according to Judge Sullivan, allow only one answer. Whether or not you agree with him, he will stimulate your little gray cells all the way.

And now for two action thrillers both worth your attention.

Imperturbable Peter Craig, British Foreign Office, dominates Kenneth Benton’s **Craig and the Jaguar** (Walker, $5.95), this time on a mission to Peru for M.I.6. He must rescue Jack Warne, young Englishman whose disappearance from the mountains, together with the stealing of Warne’s report, disturbs British authority.

“Jaguar” refers to the fabled cat-god of the Andes, symbolized more by a feline-shaped mountain than by vicious Don Jorge Santa Cruz, dispossessed tycoon with a terrorist army. To rescue Jack Warne, as well as the girl who loves him, Craig literally flies in the face of the cat-god for a slam-bang, bullet-torn finish in which all honest souls can be happy.

For contemporary dirty work in northern Ireland, its own title best describes **The Savage Day**, by Jack Higgins (Fawcett, 95¢).

Major Simon Vaughan, formerly British Army, has been first railroaded to prison, then promised pardon if he will trap I.R.A. Leader Michael Cork and recover half a million in stolen gold.

On an expedition never straightforward but always deadly, aided by Norah Murphy and (of all people) by Michael Cork too, Vaughan faces treachery or crossfire from Belfast to Dublin and back again. He is still meeting surprises when a final surprise twist leaves us gasping but satisfied as we close the book.
