THE JURY BOX
by JOHN DICKSON CARR

Quite unintentionally, be assured, I blundered and misled you. It is hard to understand in what strange absence of mind or loss of mind I called A.A. Milne the only writer ever elected to the London Detection Club on the strength of one novel alone. And I did it twice, in successive months, without realizing what had been said. If I looked up from the typewriter, I could have seen among books against the opposite wall a copy of E.C. Bentley’s Trent’s Last Case.

Trent’s Last Case had established itself as a detective classic before the outbreak of World War I. When Anthony Berkeley founded the Detection Club in 1932, E.C. Bentley became a charter member. Our club’s first president, the great G.K. Chesterton, died four years later; and every member voted for Bentley to succeed him. Though during that same year ’36 the creator of Philip Trent wrote Trent’s Own Case in collaboration with H. Warner Allen, and afterwards provided the Strand Magazine with a short-story series collected as Trent Intervenes, at the time he was elected club president by acclamation he could show only the solitary, blazing beacon of 1913.

Edmund Clerihew Bentley (1875-1956) had many talents. We who knew him remember him well: blurly, quiet-voiced, always with a twinkle in his eye. He wrote Greedy Night, that burlesque of a Lord Peter Wimsey adventure which remains at once the funniest and most ingenious parody extant, being both parody and capsule detective story with all the clues. He also invented the casual, adroit verse-form called a clerihew. Thus:

“Mr. Cecil B. De Mille,
Sorely against his will,
Was persuaded to leave Moses
Out of the Wars of the Roses.”
