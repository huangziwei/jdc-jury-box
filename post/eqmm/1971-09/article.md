## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

This time I was almost cornered, or thought I should probably be cornered at the last moment, and feared I might have to fob you off with excuses or gobbledegook. Until nearly deadline only one choice item had appeared out of the blue. Though this has happened before, and it might have seemed simple to support the single new title with three first-class old ones, offhand I could think either of no choice old items or of none that would readily be available if you tried to find them.

Then, of sudden, they all hove in sight at once.

Now, in the corral after the roundup, with satisfaction I can point you out three brilliant detective novels—two new, one the hardcover reprint of a beauty from two decades gone by—but each of classic pattern and strict fair play. These three novels will be backed up with a well-chosen anthology of the best mystery and suspense stage-plays for forty-odd years. Let’s consider.

At the beginning you may think that **Remote Control**, by Harry Carmichael (McCall, $4.50), is unlikely to prove a classic detective novel. Despite all the emotion, which begins swirling at once in a Fleet Street pub and has not yet reached its thickest even when country tragedy intervenes, the author’s approach seems almost too casual, even too careless or unbuttoned, to fit in with any classic pattern.

If you think that, you will wake up with a bump. It is the most legitimate of dirty work; every move has been craftily done to deceive. When Hugh Melville is sent to prison for running down his neighbor in that fatal accident, Mrs. Melville apparently kills herself. What is the reason for this seeming suicide, with its equally curious circumstances? With our detectives Quinn, *Morning Post*, Inspector Byram, Buckinghamshire C.I.D., and John Piper, Insurance Investigator, we follow not only the tragic Melvilles, but tragic and attractive Judith King, embittered widow of the accident victim. Past a nightmare, through emotion or excitement at their thickest, we emerge at last into a solution as daring as it is simple, as ingenious as it is unexpected. Highly recommended.

In Hugh Pentecost’s **The Deadly Joke** (Dodd, Mead, $4.95) that reliable old pro has fashioned another murderous record from the history of the Beaumont, New York’s supreme luxury hotel, at which gifted Pierre Chambrun is both manager and connoisseur of crime. Having undertaken politics, wealthy Douglas Maxwell appears at the hotel for what seems a tasteless joke, wearing white tie and tails but only underdrawers without trousers; he is shot dead in public by some unknown hand.

But it wasn’t Maxwell; it was somebody impersonating him. Police headaches multiply. Was Maxwell meant as victim, or his impersonator? Motives, suspects flash everywhere; our murderer has caused almost a holocaust before Chambrun himself solves the problem to prevent injustice. Mr. Pentecost has done it again.

First published in 1950, **Frequent Hearses**, by Edmund Crispin (London House & Maxwell, $4.95), marks the welcome reappearance of Gervase Fen, Oxonian don and amateur detective. Dirty work at a British film studio offers rich fields for satire and for classic detection. At Long Fulton we get both, plus literate characters as well as fast action, in groping through a formidable maze of murder from the first obvious suicide to its absolute opposite at the end.

Some books improve with age, acquiring a certain deadly charm over the years. The author has gone on from strength to strength, but he has done nothing better. If you missed this notable box of tricks first time round, be sure you don’t miss it now.

**Best Mystery and Suspense Plays of the Modern Theatre**, with Prefaces and an Introductory Note by Stanley Richards (Dodd, Mead, $10.00), carries us as far back as *Dracula* or *The Letter* in 1927, and as far forward as *Sleuth*, still holding on at this writing. If a classic mystery appears only in *The Witness for the Prosecution*, by Agatha Christie, we are gripped with hypnotic diabolism—both by Patrick Hamilton’s *Angel Street*, which I saw in London under the title of *Gaslight*, and by Maxwell Anderson’s *Bad Seed*.

Whether it be ghoulish comedy (Joseph Kesselring’s *Arsenic and Old Lace*) or fantasy-suspense (J.B. Priestley’s *Dangerous Corner*) all these items have grip; they seize and won’t let go. Should you have any prejudice against reading plays, accept my assurance: take the plunge here and forever you will be cured.
