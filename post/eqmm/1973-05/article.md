## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Two detective novels, one spy story, one cops-and-robbers chronicle landing its sneak punch before the bell. That’s the tally; let your mentor begin with yet another personal confession.

When lack of worthy new books compels me to recommend an old one, I don’t mind. Conscience will squirm, hoot, or gibber only when the old book in question is not readily available. With ten more Agatha Christie titles now issued in paperback, conscience can remain quiet. And there has been no evasive waffling about “suspense.” Apart from some few novels without famous sleuth, each Poirot or Miss Marple adventure is honorably called a murder mystery, as it ought to be.

Of Hercule Poirot, “I’m sick of the little blighter!” his *creatrix* remarked one night at dinner. It startles me to recall that she said this fully thirty-five years ago. No doubt she has since become reconciled; we others have liked and admired the Belgian with the moustaches since his first appearance in 1920.

Among ten titles reissued, Dame Agatha’s **Third Girl** (Pocket Books, 95¢) claims pride of place because it must rank with the best. Norma Restarick, who can’t be sure whether or not she committed a murder, first calls on Poirot and then bolts away into hiding. Does the secret concern Norma’s wealthy father, her new stepmother, old Sir Roderick Horsefield, or the loutish young artist she may marry? What of the two girls with whom she shares a flat, or of one victim fallen to death from the seventh floor? Aided by Mrs. Ariadne Oliver, that eminent writer, Poirot sifts clues in yet another victory for the little gray cells.

Don’t be alarmed at the heavily academic atmosphere of **The Serpent Under It**, by Edith Taylor (Norton, $5.95). This viper (see Shakespeare, as also for the names of the cats) menaces Hoyt College when Anne Redmond, wife of an English instructor, finds Professor Archibald stabbed in that sunlit garden of peace.

Though the author does not spare us the oldest English Department jokes, she provides new jokes too; she provides expert plotting and vivid characterization, from faculty to graduate students to interfering females allied with administration. One more murder, poor What'shername's, speeds the action towards its dramatic end. And all the evidence is there.

We must have more campus dirty work from a baffler of such obvious talent. In non-academic terms, let it firmly be stated, Miss Taylor has plenty of class.

For those of us who lived in England not only throughout Hitler's War but during those uneasy, espionage-haunted nineteen fifties, **The Spy Trap**, by Burton Graham (Weybright and Talley, $4.95), strikes a reminiscent chord. Mystery has always surrounded the death of Commander Crabb, famous British frogman, who may have got too close to a visiting Russian warship. Modern as today's newspaper, this story harks back.

Michael Evans, cloak-and-dagger brigade, runs slap into attempted murder at every turn. The German popsy of Author's Agent Stringer has promised him a manuscript with the truth about Commander Crabb if he can whisk that manuscript past Soviet Security. Whereupon Evans, Stringer, American Newswoman Sara Collins, a Russian defector, and sinister Kovarsky of sinister Department S thud together at homicidal football with unknown X in the backfield.

Climaxing a wild chase following that last confrontation at the Polish farm, Evans and the Russian defector must batter their way *into* East Germany before they can get out again, which suitably concludes this first novel of promise already fulfilled.

In **A Matter of Confidence**, by Brad Williams and J. W. Erlich (Holt, Rinehart & Winston, $4.95), professional writer and formidable courtroom lawyer have produced mystery-adventure loaded with high explosive. The jade chess set smuggled out of Red China for Millionaire Antoine Straus may or may not be Persian masterwork more than a thousand years old. Death and destruction follow at once.

The flavor of San Francisco's Chinatown drifts through each incident like fog from the bay, bedevilling attorney Sam Benedict. In court Sam must prove that the shooting of Johnnie Wung was no murder; out of court he must show who strangled the airline hostess at the Airline Hotel. Despite mayhem and arson from young hoods uncontrolled by the Chinese Six Companies, proof seems elusive until a certain person's mask drops during a burst of gun play at the end. First class.
