## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

More than once, even in the midst of griping, I have indicated what to these jaundiced eyes would seem my ideal four books for any given month. This lineup, high quality being understood, would consist of three fair-play detective novels and one roaring adventure in espionage. Such luck is hard to envision. Yet sometimes, incredibly, the miracle occurs; the scissors snip a rounded pattern; the old reprobate gets his wish.

Well, here it is again.

At first, in **Murder Without Icing**, by Emma Lathen (Simon and Schuster, $5.95), you may scarcely recognize the dignified old Sloan Guaranty Trust of Wall Street. As television sponsors of the New York Huskies, a professional hockey team now surging from cellar position towards the Stanley Cup, they have chosen well.

But problems trip up every move. Sabotage appears certain when a Nashville businessman, maneuvering to buy the franchise from Mrs. “Clemmie” Post and young Win Holland, is shot to death at La Guardia Airport. Then poison claims Billy Siragusa, their star, at the height of a crucial game. Once more, Banker-Detective John Putnam Thatcher must sift through almost too much evidence for the grisly secret *au fond*.

Under the author’s indulgent yet satiric eye we meet all our old friends: Charlie Trinkham, Everett Gabler, Brad Withers, Miss Corsa. We meet the Huskies, their fans, their coach, and their wives. All honor to Emma Lathen! Whether at ice hockey or at sound plotting, America’s First Lady of Detection has well and truly scored again.

With Hercule Poirot proving that **Elephants Can Remember**, by Agatha Christie (Dodd, Mead, $6.95), Great Britain’s First Lady of Detection offers her eighty-second novel in eighty-two years. For more than fifty of those years Poirot has united true hearts as well as detected murderers, his moustaches like a banner of chivalry.

Here he must investigate past mystery, when General Sir Alistair Ravenscroft and Lady Ravenscroft both died by violence on the cliff. Murder plus suicide, evidently; but which of them first killed the other? For the Ravenscrofts’ daughter, who is also goddaughter of Detective Novelist Ariadne Oliver, now wants to get married.

Can Hercule Poirot, summoned by Mrs. Oliver, banish old shades of scandal? We know he can; we know he will. And Dame Agatha, infinitely various with stratagems, has lost none of her liveliness or her skill at bamboozling the unwary.

Though certain motivation for Ivon Baker’s **Grave Doubt** (McKay-Washburn, $4.95) may also begin long ago, it cumulates today in several gruesome enigmas and strong emotional impact.

Young David Meynell, military archaeologist, has been employed to oversee the salvage of a Heinkel bomber downed in England during Hitler’s War. One German airman’s grave is empty; his body has disappeared. Another important witness, after surviving the Heinkel’s fate for thirty years, dies in a mysterious car-crash while driving the wrong way.

The parish, whose lord of the manor is also its vicar, becomes dangerous ground both for David and for Anne Dowland, the American heroine. Investigation sharpens as Inspector Backus narrows the field; David, escaping a fiery trap, nails the right murderer by accusing the wrong one at the right time.

The author, an Anglican clergyman, can be realistic without obscenity or any descent to the crude. It’s a pleasure to welcome this newcomer; **Grave Doubt** must rank with the best.

In **Assignment—Maltese Maiden**, by Edward S. Aarons (Fawcett, 75¢), the latest cloak-and-dagger broil carries Sam Durell from northern Africa to the island of Malta, and at quite literally breakneck pace.

General Dickinson McFee, Sam’s C.I.A. boss, has been kidnapped by Mao’s sinister, sadistic Madame Hung, as tireless at dirty work as Fu-Manchu before the era of ping-pong. General McFee must be rescued before he cracks. Parts played by Maltese Maiden Anne-Marie, by Deirdre Paget, and by Colonel Skoll of the Russian K.G.B., all fit neatly into explosive adventure with a twist at the end. First-rate Aarons, meaning top-grade Durell.
