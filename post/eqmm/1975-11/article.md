## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Whatever may be the weather outside, this magazine’s calendar shows November. And so, with your permission, let’s glance back over the worthiest titles in '75. Last year, for some reason which now escapes me, your mentor summed up rather late. This year, due to certain publishing exigencies, it seems advisable to sum up rather early.

On the whole, then, we’ve had great good luck. The new books, though far from plentiful, have been of such very high quality that more than one must rank as memorable. Let’s devote ourselves to new items, leaving reprints for briefer survey at the end.

Detection first. *Reverend Randollph and the Wages of Sin*, by Charles Merrill Smith, introduces Dr. C. P. Randollph, Chicago’s Protestant parson-sleuth, with dirty work whose pace, vividness, humor, and fair play end in a double-surprise solution of which even the expert will anticipate only half. Don’t miss it.

Also sound investigation, Alan Hunter’s *Gently with the Ladies* again features Chief Superintendent George Gently, Scotland Yard, in a luxury-flat murder which, despite so many women at emotional conflict, will please the most exacting fan.

For the poisoning problems of *You’re Never Too Old To Die*, by Arthur D. Goldstein, a retired businessman turned amateur detective finds baser passions flaming as high in his Senior Citizens’ Center as anywhere among humankind. Unassuming, excellent.

One year’s tidewater flood of the River Severn, called Severn Bore, provides its title for *Bored to Death*, by Michael Delving, in which Dave Cannon, American art dealer exploring rural England, finds mediaeval treasure trove and traps an ingenious murderer to straighten out several tangles you will thoroughly enjoy.

Also enjoyable, Hugh Pentecost’s *Time of Terror* shows Pierre Chambrun heavily at odds with lethal hocus-pocus threatening in more than one sense to wreck his luxury hotel.

No addict need even be told he will enjoy Emma Lathen’s *By Hook or by Crook*, wherein Banker Detective Thatcher once more saves faces and sanity when the Sloan Guaranty Trust meets misadventure with Parajians, Inc., their fine Oriental rugs, and a fine old feast of homicide.

Mystery thrillers? If you crave excitement, try *The Woodcutter Operation*, by Kenneth Royce, in which armed criminals capture a great London hospital until inspiration finds our answer for the most explosive scene of all.

David Westheimer's *The Avila Gold*; though less violent, also grips fast with its frantic treasure-hunt underneath Los Angeles and its triumphant hero, mild-seeming Professor McDowell, UCLA.

No current heist, *The Great Train Robbery*, by Michael Crichton, brilliantly tells how a Victorian master criminal swooped down on gold meant for British troops in the Crimea.

Vividly studying morbid patterns, William P. McGivern's *Night of the Juggler* traces Gus Soltik, child-rapist and killer, from his first vicious outbreak to the snare in Central Park.

And now for espionage. In *The Man with Two Clocks*, by Whit Masterson, well-meaning young Michael Lord escapes charges of treason only through exploits during which clue after clue builds towards a surprise wallop at the end.

If Robin Close's *The Bohème Combination* seems mainly a combination of crooks, it needs great skill in the British Treasury man who must foil Soviet plots despite treachery at home.

In *The Judas Sheep*, by Jan Roberts, a London art dealer turned American agent just survives his peril-fanged mission to Greece.

Michael Bar-Zohar's *The Spy Who Died Twice* hurls Jeff Saunders, CIA, into an international nightmare until Jeff's study of evidence reveals answers as unexpected as they are satisfactory.

Even if space permitted, it would be cumbersome to recall all the good paperbacks. But my own favorites deserve mention.

You'll enjoy Emma Lathen's *Murder Against the Grain*, Brian Garfield's *The Threepersons Hunt*, Lionel White's *The Mexico Run*, Desmond Bagley's *The Tightrope Men*, Bill Pronzini's *Snowbound*, Graham Greene's *The Confidential Agent*, and *A Prayer for the Dying* or *Night Judgment at Sinos*, both by Jack Higgins.

Preoccupied, I have almost neglected two new books, further prize items: *Ellery Queen's Aces of Mystery*, his latest anthology, and Antonia Fraser's *King James VI of Scotland and I of England*, a life of that royal grotesque innocently concerned with so much crime. Yes, a very successful year.
