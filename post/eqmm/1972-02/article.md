## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

If prejudice may be allowed, very heartily I now dislike and distrust that word “suspense.” No doubt it had a certain cogency when Miss Kahn first applied it to the admirable Harper list. But today almost every attempt at mystery has come, rightly or wrongly, to be so described. If it’s a good mystery, that’s very high praise; let the publisher say so, disdaining weaker terms. Though I myself prefer “detective” novel, the highest praise, this word may be used only when there has been complete fair play with clues, a feature less rare than some critics appear to think.

Again we are to consider four titles, three novels and a book of unsolved enigmas from real life. Of the three fictions, one first novel and two second tries, each is a first-class mystery which plays strictly fair. At one stride, then, we have a whole trio of new candidates for homicidal honors. With great pleasure the old pastrycook welcomes them to the bakery.

Bill Pronzini’s **The Snatch** (Random House, $5.95) is told in the first person by a private investigator whose name we never learn, but whom we can welcome with the heartiest handshake. Though he professes admiration for the ancient pulp fictions of Hammett and Chandler, this unnamed sleuth bears no resemblance to dull, swollen-headed snarlers like Spade or Marlowe, whose famed “toughness” was mainly bad manners and who cultivated an unpleasant personality as their stock in trade. Mistakenly called Old Bear by his nagging girl friend, our protagonist forms an immensely likeable addition to the roster; it seems we shall meet him again.

When the nine-year-old son of Financial Speculator Louis Martinetti is kidnapped, with a ransom demand for $300,000, the boy’s frantic father has been warned that no member of the family must carry this money to any drop. In semi-isolation on the Peninsula with his wife and his few friends—Dean Proxmire, secretary, and Allan Channing, that fish-blooded tycoon—Martinetti summons Mr. No Name from San Francisco to make the drop.

Most kidnapping stories follow a conventional pattern. This one explodes in every new direction of twist and violence, with Mr. Nameless following Mr. Faceless down dark, dangerous roads to a neatly prepared surprise end. Not quite the author's maiden effort, but mark it A plus.

In **The Detective Wore Silk Drawers**, by Peter Lovesey (Dodd, Mead, $4.95), Mr. Lovesey follows last year's *Wobble to Death* with a second venture into the world of late Victorian sport: this time no walking race at a pace designed to kill, but the brutal, still more deadly prize ring in 1880.

Well-born, well-educated Metropolitan Police Constable Henry Jago, noted amateur boxer, must probe the riddle of a headless, unidentified pugilist found in the Thames. At Radstock Hall, under the supervision of Robert D'Estin and fascinating Isabel Vibart, they train the amateur to exhaustion for his bloody, bare-knuckle encounter with an unbeatable professional. After murder has visited Radstock Hall itself, Sergeant Cribb of Scotland Yard fits together the solution and smashes a vicious racket in this vivid period-piece of *mores* long gone-by.

Though the mystery in **The Murders on the Square**, by Theodore George (Dodd, Mead, $4.95), is solved less by ratiocination than by routine police work, none the less it provides an ingenious detective story supported by visible evidence.

In quiet St. Paul's Square, lower Manhattan, four girls of very different types are messily slain by a Jack-the-Ripper kind of menace on the loose. Lieutenant Zimmerman and Sergeant Donofrio, those very humane policemen, must nail their quarry amid gathering panic. If the identity of the murderer proves far from a shock finale, this whole affair has been so expert and so convincing that we feel no disappointment. An accomplished first novel which promises even better investigations to come.

Dealing in the main with unexplained deaths through the years, from Amy Robsart in 1560 to Dag Hammarskjold only a decade ago, with problems of identity in doubt or of men and women who vanished, Kenneth B. Platnick's **Great Mysteries of History** (Stackpole, $6.95) in sixteen well-presented cases also takes a look at lost continents, at inexplicable presences from the Abominable Snowman to the Loch Ness Monster, and at other factual puzzles as well. Here are the various answers; choose any or none. For, being myself a student of the unsolved picturesque, I strongly urge you to buy, borrow, or steal this book.
