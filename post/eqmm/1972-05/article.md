## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Once more, as might have been expected, we encounter lean days. This month, it must be reported, only one new novel should merit your attraction. With reprints, however, I have had the luck of the draw. In paperback, everywhere available, you will now find three supplementary high cards: two detective classics of thirty-odd years ago, and one mystery-adventure-fantasy, with cloak-and-dagger overtones, undiminished for verve and sparkle since its first appearance in 1908.

To unravel the tangle of **And Shame the Devil**, by Sara Woods (Holt, Rinehart & Winston, $4.95), again Antony Maitland, that very sympathetic barrister-detective, champions hopeless clients as with equal skill or quixotry he juggles a hot potato.

Arkenshaw, Yorkshire, has racial (*i.e.*, prejudice?) problems not unlike our own. Two Pakistanis, newcomers to this hard-headed community, have been arrested on a charge of burglary by dour Sergeant Duckett and young Constable Ryder. Acquitted by the court, Ghulam Beas and Chakwal Mohamad bring suit for false arrest.

Briefed by the defense, Antony Maitland encounters storms domestic as well as legal. Admitting prejudice, Sergeant Duckworth, supported by his handsome daughter and fiery “Grandma,” all the same seems an honest man. But what of the evidence against him?

Though at first Maitland can see no villain, some shape of evil becomes evident when courtroom tension explodes in a poisoning mystery on the very doorstep of the law. Aided by none other than distinguished opposing counsel, Kevin O’Brien, Maitland fits together every clue. Once more Miss Woods, with all her usual ingenuity and charm of style, has written a detective novel which connoisseurs must read.

Originally published in 1931, **The Rynox Murder** by Philip MacDonald (Avon, 95¢) strikes a note of the topsy-turvy from its startling first scene to its equally startling end. Prologue and epilogue seem to have been turned the wrong way round.

At once we are involved with F. X. Benedik, financial wizard, as well as F. X.'s son and various associates, including delectable Petronella. Then the fusillade of shots in quiet Kensington, the body across the window-sill, touch off a pyrotechnic problem which dazzles eyesight.

There is no official investigator; you yourself are the sleuth for a deception so full of tricks that you must watch every step. Mr. MacDonald, past master at devising pratfalls, uses fair play itself to hoodwink us as he shows that the topsy-turvy may also be the right side up. Thus, as always, Q.E.D.

Though your mentor would never hold that early stories are always best stories, **The League of Frightened Men** by Rex Stout (Pyramid, 75¢) features Gargantuan Nero Wolfe in an early exploit which has always been my favorite.

The men of the title, once college classmates, are for the most part worthy citizens, solid and successful. Yet they walk in fear of a revengeful outcast, Paul Chapin, whom their undergraduate prank has crippled for life. The judge fell to death from a cliff; the art dealer drank poison; the psychologist has vanished. Though murder can't be proved, their unearthly cripple boasts of it. Prospective victims will pay Nero Wolfe a large sum—Wolfe himself has so maneuvered matters—if he can save them from Chapin and remove the menace.

The sage of West 35th Street, enthroned behind his desk, has never been more impressive. He points out another side to the gospel of violence; he proves who killed whom and why; he picks up the marbles. Both Wolfe and his creator being past masters of hoodwinkery, they have combined their talents in a true classic.

In my boyhood, before I had met Father Brown, G. K. Chesterton's **The Man Who Was Thursday** (Ballantine, 95¢) introduced me to the writer who became my literary idol. Though subtitled "a nightmare," it glitters with the wit, the vividness, the excitement, the ingenuity of G.K.C.'s detective fiction; even its fantasy is rooted in common sense.

Gabriel Syme, poet-detective, must track down a conspiracy of anarchists whose seven leaders have been named for the days of the week. Hilarious high jinks carry him from his first challenge to the chase through London with a fleet of cabs, a fire-engine, an elephant, and a balloon. Whatever the philosophic meaning, you will find it glorious entertainment.
