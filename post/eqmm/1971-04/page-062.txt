THE JURY BOX
by JOHN DICKSON CARR

A change in the format of this magazine will mean less space in which the skipper of your friendly column may pontificate. Henceforward, instead of the three pages allowed me for almost a year, we shall revert to the chaste two pages of yore.

Yet to be so restricted can offer positive advantages. There will be more space for appraising the books themselves, and such enforced conciseness should effectually curb your mentor’s congenital fondness for talking too much.

We begin, at least, with a good omen. This month, thank fate, I can offer you four titles, all new, all as expertly contrived as smoothly written: three classic detective novels and one strong mystery-adventure with detective overtones too. Equally divided between British and American, they have been written by a trio of homicidal ladies and one murderous-minded gentleman at the top of his form.

With A Guilty Thing Surprised, by Ruth Rendell (Crime Club, $4.50), Mrs. Rendell more than lives up to the reputation for ingenuity and craft she has established in seven earlier novels.

Despite the outwardly placid life of Elizabeth and Quentin Nightingale at Myfield Manor during that golden country weather, decorative Elizabeth goes once too often into the darkling woods near the house, and for no apparent reason is brutally slain. It upsets the husband with whom she gets on almost too well; it even upsets the brother with whom she can’t get on at all, and stills the incessant giggling of an au pair girl from Holland. Chief Inspector Wexford, that able investigator we have met before, guides his less sensitive colleague, Inspector Burden, amid evidence as twisted as the emotions behind each move.
