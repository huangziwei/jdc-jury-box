## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Despite my recurring gripe that mystery mongers now dazzle their fans with *almost* everything except mystery, tolerant interpretation of the rules will allow us to include the present month’s crop in this category. A story may be classed as mystery, perhaps, if the outcome of the hullabaloo remains in doubt until our author chooses to reveal it.

On this basis, then, I give you four sizzlers among the cops-and-robbers, with several surprises along the way, ranging from massive raid on city or art treasure to the epic gambling-house heist of them all. Don’t carp; relax and enjoy.

Parker, the amoral protagonist of **Butcher’s Moon**, by Richard Stark (Random House, $4.95), seems not so much lacking in human weakness as utterly devoid of all human feeling. Does he like food, wine, women, or anything else? We don’t know; he is thief, strategist, murderer, whom we must accept as forever unrepentant and uncaught.

In the midwestern city of Tyler for hidden plunder some other crook has lifted from him, Parker strikes back lethally to recover his loot. Aided by Alan Grofield, his actorish young partner, he tangles at once with undercover forces ever more murderous as the battle waxes in heat.

At times it seems less cops and robbers than straight robbed and robbers, one of the robbers being an important cop. When they wound and capture Grofield, Parker summons more partners for the mass attack which becomes his own bomber’s moon, and recovers enough cash for reasonable compensation. You won’t much like the fellow, but he’ll fascinate you all the way.

In **Pandora’s Box**, by Thomas Chastain (Mason & Lipscomb, $6.95), six priceless paintings from the Metropolitan Museum of Art are stolen and held for ransom in a heist planned by Joe Conant, black young war veteran with a law degree from Columbia.

Opposing sophisticated Conant stands wealthy, sophisticated Deputy Chief Inspector Max Kauffman, N.Y.P.D., Rhodes scholar and also a lawyer as well. But Kauffman can’t be everywhere. It’s a daring and ingenious robbery, not least daring or ingenious in the hiding place for the stolen paintings, which will wallop the most experienced reader with its legitimate surprise punch.

Well, let’s not anticipate. The aching tug of war between cops and robbers holds one more surprise before the end. Still, since we have been led to sympathize both with Inspector Kauffman for the cops and Joe Conant for the robbers, the penultimate burst of gunplay might have ended worse. If you want expert storytelling as well as sound characterization and credible psychology, don’t miss this one.

Ex-Major Patrick Clancy, formerly American Army, survives more perils than mysteries in John Murphy’s **The El Greco Puzzle** (Scribners, $6.95), whose action blazes across three continents and much of civilization.

Living in Paraguay, sent to Spain as agent for an exiled French millionaire of dubious repute, Clancy undertakes to return with the smallish El Greco studies which were not taken in Napoleon’s time but must be spirited away now.

Can Clancy do it? He is irked by French authority, hounded by predators, jeered at rather than aided by Susan Allgood, the captivating art adviser who says she hates him. Even Marshal Massena had no such task; the cards seem hopelessly stacked.

How Clancy *suffered* a change of mind after Susan has suffered a change of heart; how he voted with the angels at San Ildefonso Monastery; how chief predator and stooges get their comeuppance: all these climax an action thriller every reader can enjoy.

A temple-of-chance complex called The Klondike roars as background for **Las Vegas**, by Arthur Moore and Clayton Matthews (Pocket Books, $1.50). Each of those present—worried manager, honeymoon couple, Texas millionaire, pilfering dealer-girl, Mafia agent—has some part to play, even if mere supernumerary, in a king-size robbery which pries open the Klondike’s strongroom.

Here’s one hell of an upset. The few sympathetic characters emerge with honor; we need have no fear for Brent, the manager, or for Billie Ray, the Texan relaxing with stud poker and blondes. Though the authors might have spared us certain crudities of speech during their heavy emphasis on sex, the plotting is so dovetailed and the whole picture so convincing that it holds its pace until the end.
