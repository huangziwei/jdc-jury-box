## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

If your mentor seldom leads his month’s choices with a book of short stories, the custom arises only from convenience in reserving such items for the end. This time, however, we shall begin with a short-story selection whose quality deserves pride of place in any company, despite the backing of three worthy novels.

**Best “Thinking Machine” Detective Stories**, by Jacques Futrelle, edited by E. F. Bleiler (Dover, $3.00), will be manna for judicious readers. Jacques Futrelle, that one-time journalistic wizard of the Boston *American*, wrote two volumes of brilliantly ingenious tales featuring Professor Augustus S. F. X. Van Dusen, the Thinking Machine. Georgia-born, New Englander by adoption, our author was still a young man when he died with the *Titanic* disaster in 1912.

As one much influenced by his stories of impossible situations rationally explained, I have often hoped for some reissue. The present ably edited selection contains every favorite, from “The Problem of Cell 13” to others already praised in this column. You will read of impossible murder by illuminating gas; you will read of the phantom motor-car which vanished from a road between stone walls; you will read of miracles not quite so miraculous under Professor Van Dusen’s scrutiny.

There are more such adventures, it seems, to be winnowed from old newspapers and magazines. Let’s hope that crabbed little scientist will be with us soon again.

Told in the first person by Karl Berger, Stockholm newsman who has bought a fancied horse, Olle Hogstrand’s **The Gambler** (Pantheon, $4.95) offers classic detection as well as characters all too human against its vivid background of harness racing in provincial Sweden.

At the track, amid such shady operators as Professional Gambler Svensson or Ring, the pornographic publisher, Karl Berger sees the collapse of a rival’s horse touch off arson, suicide, and murder. Who planted the syringe in the cupboard? Relying only on Trainer-driver Jan Lindgren and Anette, that shapely siren, Berger feels the grip of some Scandinavian syndicate whose Yugoslav front man coils its tentacles round every vice.

Pressured with sales talk, beaten up by thugs, under police surveillance for aiding Lindgren’s hapless brother, our narrator seems lost until after a sensational race C.I.D. Superintendent Krollin nails the murderer with evidence in plain sight. Olle Hogstrand (pronounce *that* one) knows all the tricks.

With **The Highbinders**, by Oliver Bleeck (Morrow, $5.95), we join Philip St. Ives, American go-between for the return of heisted valuables, in a cops-and-robbers caper through half-world London.

The fabled thirteenth-century sword of France’s Crusader Saint, King Louis IX, has been stolen from Ned and Norbert Nitry, wealthy British con artists more or less retired. St. Ives must ransom this historic treasure through English Eddie Apex, younger con man representing some mysterious thief unknown.

Careful, now! With a hundred thousand pounds as ransom, with death at every corner, St. Ives dodges snares and booby-traps from graveyard to park to suburban shop. Bloody is the trail, bitter grow frustrations, before he finds St. Louis’s sword in a hiding place so ingenious as almost to defy detection, and cars race towards the final shoot-out on Waterloo Bridge. You can trust Ross Thomas, here alias Oliver Bleeck. It’s a rip-roaring story.

The London of R. G. Jeffreys’s **A Wicked Way to Die** (Walker, $5.95) is London in 1800, its turbulent Age of Reason having overtaken the nineteenth century. Bow Street Runner Jeremy Sturrock, who must not be confused with Bow Street’s Blackstone somewhat later, recounts his own exploits unhampered by modesty. He takes charge at once when young Robert Mytton, son of the great banker, is shot to death in an underground room beneath Mr. Sheridan’s Theatre Royal, Drury Lane.

With Sturrock’s foul-mouthed little assistant we move through pub, slum, and palace; we watch a duel in Hyde Park Fields; we visit the original Grub Street as this able if self-satisfied officer untangles the scheming of thieves, cons, and prostitutes.

Though the secret of the locked room can scarcely be called startling or unexpected, we have not seen all the cards in Mr. Jeffreys’s hand. Impatience must be restrained until Sturrock halts his quarry that night on the marshes. There, in the mist by the mill pond, one real surprise explodes amid blazing musketry to mark a satisfactory climax and a fitting end.
