## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Again, for various reasons, let’s omit preliminary remarks. We face a tricky lot this time.

Tricky, did I say? Yes, but no great disappointment. Though as a rule fortune will deal me at least one first-class detective novel for my lead, it has not happened here. On the other hand, I can recommend two mystery-adventure items of high-voltage pace and excitement, with different sorts of efforts to back them up. Well, we have fared much worse.

**Jink**, by Thomas Patrick McMahon (Simon and Schuster, $4.95) is nobody’s name. In technical language it means “violent evasive action and reaction to avoid extreme hazard from enemy fire.” Much action of all sorts must be undertaken by Major John McKendrick, that sympathetic Air Force officer against whom all circumstances conspire.

Returning to Florida after more than a year’s duty in Thailand, McKendrick finds his wife and three children mysteriously missing without a word. Authority, summoned for help, has almost decided that innocent McKendrick and Anne must be part of a jewel-smuggling ring—or worse.

McKendrick won’t take it; he fights. Figuratively, then, he is under fire not only from some sinister opposing force but from those on Our Side who seem as malignant as the enemy. After many twists and turns the battle ceases to be figurative; we sweep to a surprise finale and that spectacular confrontation in the sky. Don’t jink with this one; grab it and read.

Despite its unpromising title, Donald Mackenzie’s **Sleep Is for the Rich** (Houghton Mifflin, $5.95) proves to be one of the year’s best: paradoxically, a crime story of such hypnotic charm that it will hold you pliant to its every shading of mood through comedy, thrill, irony, and grimness to a triumphant end.

Our protagonist, Paul Henderson, literate and civilized jewel-thief from Canada, after a disastrous marriage has been left in England with a delightful seven-year-old daughter named Sophie. Though he wants to go straight for Sophie’s sake, it’s not to be. Fat-cat Harry Chalice, crude but clever mastermind at all dirty work, rich and retired but still ambitious, meditates a last coup which shall make Chalice’s name ring through history. Aided by his murderous young hanger-on, Crying Eddie, he plans the biggest jewel robbery of all time.

At an estate in Switzerland Marika Bergen, once world skating star, now an aging blonde with her fabulous collection of gems, will entertain a house party whose principal guests are famous women with famous gem collections of their own. To strip that party of its every valuable, reasons Mastermind Chalice, he needs Paul Henderson’s expertise and gentlemanly manners. So Chalice tempts, applying pressure. One good haul, Henderson knows, will assure his daughter’s future. Greatly tempted, he yields and joins in.

Then we’re at it—pull thief, pull devil—amid characters as diverse as an alluring German baroness and the blackmailer called Greasy George. We like Paul Henderson; greatly we hope he won’t come to harm, in spite of self-righteous maxims that the wrongdoer must suffer. But never mind; be of good heart, and don’t worry; the author likes him too.

In **The Golden Ball and Other Stories**, by Agatha Christie (Dodd, Mead, $5.95), we have fifteen tales written by Dame Agatha in her exuberant youth but never before republished under this title. In theme ranging from practical crime to twilight and the unseen, each story is good and all together are valuable as they show the burgeoning of that exhaustless dexterity of invention with which the author was to dazzle us in subsequent years.

Whether you are familiar or unfamiliar with these tales, dip into them. You can’t go wrong with Agatha Christie.

With **A Catalogue of Crime**, by Jacques Barzun and Wendell Hertig Taylor (Harper & Row, $18.95), two connoisseurs have provided what they rightly subtitle their ‘Reader’s Guide to the Literature of Mystery, Detection, & Related Genres.’

In this comprehensive study you will find every species: the novel, the short story, the fact-crime study, lined up for shrewd appraisal. The authors are here too, each of us, with strengths examined as well as faults or blunders too. If the critics’ judgment sometimes grows pompous, it is fair, honest, and sympathetic. Cherish this book; it deserves an honored place on your shelves.
