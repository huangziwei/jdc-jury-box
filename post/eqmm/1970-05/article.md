## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Attention, please! Beginning with this issue, under the new format above, your well-intentioned if maundering critic will be allowed more space wherein to maunder on about mystery in general and current mysteries in particular. Kindly refrain from audible groans or hisses.

“For all your constant griping about poor work,” I was told, “not once have you held up and pulverized a book by title or author. In the future, when you feel you must get after somebody, here’s your opportunity.”

And yet, except under unusual circumstances, I think I shall not avail myself of the privilege. Too well I remember words once spoken by an old acquaintance, Milward Kennedy, who more than thirty years ago reviewed for a London Sunday newspaper.

“Why should I waste my time and the reader’s,” he said, “airing all the faults of *Admirals Die Laughing* or *There’s No Place Like Homicide*? Let me find something most customers can enjoy, and ignore ineptitude not worth bothering about.”

My corollary reason for infrequent use of the bastinado does not arise, God knows, from innate kindness of heart. It arises from the quenchless hopefulness of the mystery-lover. Having devoured sensationalism since boyhood, we old-timers can recall several authors who couldn’t write, couldn’t construct, and couldn’t play fair, but could come up with some highly ingenious solution that atoned for everything. Today, tackling a story which begins badly and continues worse, no optimistic judge will fling it aside in mid-course. “Give the poor devil a chance!” whispers that inner voice. “Maybe he’s got something up his sleeve; read on and finish.”

Experience teaches that the author who trips over his own feet in chapter one is unlikely to become an expert gymnast by chapter twenty. But it did happen in those bad old days. And at least the new book has been honorably treated. *You* needn’t read the damn thing; no harm is done.

It would be pleasant to inaugurate this expanded column by naming four first-rate new novels. I can’t do that, but I can offer you three. If one of them is a paperback original modestly priced, that's all to the good. Our fourth item will be a classic detective story of the early nineteen twenties; also available at uninflated cost. Worthy paperbacks have saved the situation so often that your correspondent can feel profoundly grateful.

In **Death at the Chase**, by Michael Innes (Dodd, Mead, $4.50), retired Police Commissioner Sir John Appleby, of Long Dream Manor, during his afternoon walk encounters a mouldering mansion which might almost be called Long Sleep Manor.

At Sir John's first meeting with long-lived Martyn Ashmore, scion of the long-lived Ashmores, a great weight of stone crashes down from the roof, just missing them both. Somebody wants to kill parsimonious Martyn, either for reasons dating back to World War II, or for reasons more immediate. Though surrounded by unattractive elderly people of his own generation, the old skinflint does die (no cerebro-vascular accident, surely?) amid attractive young people who include Bobby Appleby, Sir John's son.

This essay in detection, told with all Mr. Innes's customary charm, centers round the problem of a view by firelight and of something the witness didn't quite see. You yourself may not see, but it's worth a try.

**The Pushbutton Butterfly**, by Kin Platt (Random House, $4.95) introduces us to Max Roper, semi-official security investigator, as knowing and with-it as any detective of his tribe.

Janet Sanders, the daughter of a West Coast electronics tycoon, has disappeared from her classes at Berkeley. She may be anywhere; she may be nowhere; she must be traced.

Beginning with the strangled (and also vanishing) Mexican girl who has shared Janet's apartment, Max Roper stumbles along a track of drugs, double bluff, pornographic art, and more corpses. If our investigator sometimes seems almost too desperately knowing, even smart-aleck, he needs armor against so many desperately knowing weirdos: that red-headed doll of slippery aspect, the student agitator less devoted to human beings than to agitation, the hopped-up Hell's Angel with a steel plate in his head.

Max, though with it, is often *hors de combat*. When they can't knock him out by fist or bludgeon, they manage well enough with sleep-inducing atomizers or cups of tea. But don't let the book's fast action fool you into thinking its ending will be clumsy or naive. The author has fashioned an intricate, soundly constructed labyrinth, evil minotaur and all.

That very sympathetic secret agent, Sam Durell of the C.I.A., meets his most fantastic challenge with **Assignment—White Rajah**, by Edward S. Aarons (Fawcett, 60¢). Over Pasangara, riot-torn province in Malaya, United States Navy fighter planes have roared towards the inner hills and failed to return. Where are those planes?

Officially neither pro-American nor pro-Communist, this strip of Southeast Asia has its own government under Premier Kuang. With a temporary post at the American Consulate, Sam Durell must learn what has happened to the lost planes and their pilots. But Premier Kuang plans to expel him as undesirable. Colonel Tileong wants him jailed or dead. Even George Hammond, an old C.I.A. friend, seems to be obstructing Sam's mission. Every hand is against him.

Every hand? In imperialistic days the whole area was once ruled by each male descendant of an English adventurer, Anthony Merrydale, as White Rajah of Pasangara. The present rajah—old, deposed, living on sufferance—has two grandchildren of Anglo-Malayan blood. His grand-daughter, a raving beauty with a questionable past, has dropped her English name to be known only as Pala Mir. Yet, when Durell saves her life in a riot, the girl herself offers to help.

If he can reach an old palace in a distant mountain, he may discover what he wants to know. Both Pala Mir and grandpa lend aid through the jungle, with Communist guerillas ahead and Colonel Tileong in pursuit. After learning the trick of hijacking planes without suspicion, after nailing the traitor behind it all, Sam Durell reaches a conclusion as satisfactory as his last night with Pala Mir. Any reader will be happy too.

A.A. Milne's **The Red House Mystery** (Avon, 95¢) seems almost forgotten now that so many crooked dark roads have become a roaring thruway. I read the book on its first appearance in 1922; as might have been predicted, it seemed insufficiently sensational. For the very young devotee, who judges all detectives by Sherlock Holmes and all figures of menace by Dr. Fu Manchu, anything short of exaggerated omniscience or exaggerated villainy will fail to satisfy. I have read it twice since then: I couldn't have been more mistaken.

A.A. Milne, dead these fourteen years, was the only writer whom London's Detection Club elected to full membership on the strength of one novel alone. It belongs among the first of those countryside chronicles wherein the lawn-mower's whir is cut short by a revolver shot, the detective as human as the murderer, the evidence in sight. It was a fine achievement then; it remains a fine achievement today.
