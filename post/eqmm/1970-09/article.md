## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Your correspondent’s remarks anent the London Detection Club have roused interest and (for me, at least) some embarrassment. One lady, teaching a writing course at a Midwestern college, asks what rules the members swear to observe in addition to those already quoted. With the best of intentions I have mislaid her letter and can’t answer it. If the lady in question will overlook this seeming discourtesy by sending her name and address, I shall be happy to reply *in propria persona*.

Meanwhile, let it be said that I have stated the only rules relating to craft or technique. The rest of the oath, though amusing and rather impressive when spoken with resonance, deals with matters social rather than technical. Even so, those existing rules provided hazards. Whatever name might be proposed for membership, it used to seem, somebody would always object on the grounds that the candidate either (a) didn’t play fair, or (b) couldn’t write good English.

One other factor tended to restrict membership. All of us soon learned the futility of electing anybody who for reasons of geography could attend few meetings or none. Thirty-odd years ago two obviously worthy members would have been Ngaio Marsh and Michael Innes, but neither was proposed because at that time the latter lived in Australia and the former, as she still does, in New Zealand.

Still and all, they were great days! Strong is the memory of how John Rhode and I would escort Dr. Austin Freeman up and down the stairs to the club rooms in Gerrard Street, hoping he wouldn’t die of a heart attack on the way to dinner at Kettner’s.

Codes of rules for constructing detective fiction have been drawn up both by the late Willard Huntington Wright (S. S. Van Dine) and by the late Monsignor Ronald Knox, himself a Detection Club member. Monsignor Knox set forth unanswerably that the crime must never be fastened on any character whose thoughts we have been allowed to share; the creator of Philo Vance said it must never be fastened on any servant. The latter point, though perhaps debatable, has much in its support. No character can be developed *as* a character when he has little more to say than, “Dinner is served,” or, “The hack’s here.”

But, whatever may be the ultimate rules, I can testify with pleasure that this month’s haul consists of three first-rate detective novels and one mystery-adventure well worth your attention.

Somewhat deceptive is the leisurely beginning of **The Best Man to Die**, by Ruth Rendell (Crime Club, $4.50). There will be fireworks ere long, erupting all over the domestic atmosphere of Kingsmarkham, Sussex.

Anybody known as “good old Charlie” Somebody, of course, will almost certainly meet a sticky end. Good old Charlie Hatton, after that bachelor party for Jack Pertwee, has met a watery end in the river before many people begin to suspect the truth about him. What of all that money Charlie has been accumulating? What of the mysterious girl who calls herself Nora Fanshawe? And how does the nursing-home really figure in this?

Chief Inspector Wexford, stolid only on the surface, attacks a problem in which everything seems to be upside down and often is. Here we have more than competent detection, told in smooth, vivid, ironic prose. Nothing is more deceptive than its finale; the character of the murderer, if you are alert enough to spot his identity, has been developed on-stage and under bright light. And Mrs. Rendell, though married to a public relations consultant, invariably plays fair.

When two well-paid fun girls share a Fun City apartment in **Finish Me Off**, by Hillary Waugh (Doubleday, $5.95), the murder of Linda Howard, *nee* Mary Bowman—stabbed to death with one of her clients at the Addison Hotel—draws Detective Frank Sessions into the victim’s murky half-world of pimps, pushers, and prostitutes.

Though Linda Howard really enjoyed this profession, her roommate, Gloria Henchel or Ruth Little, has been laboring only so that she can retire and open a beauty parlor in her native New Haven. Convinced that Gloria must know something, Frank Sessions concentrates on her when every other trail seems to end in a blind alley.

His tenacity earns its reward, if almost too late. The only deception here practised is the legitimate deception used by the criminal against authority. The trapping of this innocent-seeming killer highlights a soundly constructed murder mystery of strong characterizations and convincing background detail.

As with our next author’s other books, **Enquiry**, by Dick Francis (Harper & Row, $4.95), gives us the very breath of British steeplechase racing, even when we are far away from the track.

Kelly Hughes, steeplechase jockey who has pursued other studies at the London School of Economics, loses his license for “pulling back” a horse which has merely tired and faltered; Dexter Cranfield, the horse’s trainer, suffers the same disgrace. Somebody unknown has framed them both. The trainer, hardly a very likeable sort, is too crushed to move. Unless Kelly’s native wits can find and convict the person responsible for that frame, the future holds no prospect at all.

Colorful characters surround him: Tony, his bibulous cousin; Roberta Cranfield, the trainer’s daughter; a shady private eye named Oakley; hostile Lord Gowery of the Jockey Club; tough-minded but sympathetic Lord Ferth. Kelly’s position grows desperate when the unknown plotter tries to kill him and nearly succeeds. At length the plotter is snared, after a wild scene in which someone other than the plotter goes raving mad; and it ends, to everybody else’s satisfaction, with Kelly comfortably in the arms of a girl he has thought he didn’t like.

Near the beginning of Stanley Ellin’s **The Bind** (Random House, $5.95) we learn that it *may* have been an accident when healthy, wealthy Walter Thoren, happily married patriarch with two grown children and a life-insurance policy carrying double indemnity, has died in a car smash that should net his heirs two hundred thousand dollars. Insurance Investigator Jake Dekker doesn’t believe in accident; neither does Guaranty Life. The company will pay Jake heavily if he can prove Thoren engineered his own death to leave his family in clover; if it can’t be proved, they won’t pay Jake a penny.

Landing at Miami Beach with a problem on his mind and a temperamental “wife” on his hands, Jake wades neck-deep through trouble before he can even begin to investigate. Evil forces seem to be closing in on Thoren’s widow, his attractive daughter, and his philandering son. But the greatest enigma has been provided by the late Walter Thoren himself, a human secret on his own. The secrets are disclosed; the evil forces close in; a gory *dénouement* rounds out this brilliant mystery-adventure at top speed all the way.
