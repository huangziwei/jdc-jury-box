## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Due to the grotesquely inflated price of hardcover books, as there has been previous occasion to remark, it’s always a pleasure when I can recommend four paperbacks. And once more the miracle occurs, properly proportioned for balance.

Beginning with a pair of sound detective novels, the first a classic from the Golden Age and the second comparatively recent, we find them supported by one last season’s action thriller and one brand-new adventure of a favorite secret agent. Though chance supplied those proportions, design could have done no better.

**Keeper of the Keys**, by Earl Derr Biggers (Bantam, $1.25), is the last of the half-dozen Charlie Chan novels which Charlie’s creator wrote before his untimely death in 1933. Though I missed this story on its first appearance, our Chinese sleuth from Hawaii, with his wise saws and his unruffled temper, can be welcomed soon or late.

The four ex-husbands of Ellen Landini, beautiful but unstable opera star, have assembled at Dudley Ward’s luxurious home above Lake Tahoe. Ward, her first husband, seeks the lost son he thinks he has sired by Landini. Others, from husbands or servants to her latest *fiancé*, have different views. Amid a boiling of motives, suspects, and contradictory clues, Ellen Landini is shot to death near the study window.

If **Keeper of the Keys** cannot quite match **Charlie Chan Carries On** (also available from Bantam), it’s an ingenious, fair-play mystery remarkable for expert storytelling in this inept age. Pit your wits against the redoubtable Charlie; I venture to bet you’ll agree.

Emma Lathen’s **Murder Against the Grain** (Pocket Books, $1.25), which I also missed on its original publication eight years ago, concerns a Russian grain deal imagined some time before our best businessmen were conned by this project in reality.

“America’s Agatha Christie,” who in writing good detection can also make finance almost intelligible to one with no head for it, again ensnares us into the affairs of the Sloan Guaranty Trust, where we investigate with Banker Detective John Putnam Thatcher.

Though sinister wings take flight with a huge theft of Soviet funds and with murder outside the Russian Embassy, the author's skill at satiric comedy makes this a drama of cross-purposes in which everybody misunderstands everybody else until Thatcher and one unexpected ally fit together the clues for its airport finale. If only a connoisseur will be likely to fasten guilt where guilt belongs, that's as it should be. Every Emma Lathen title belongs to the connoisseur; for him these books are written.

Cut off in storm-cum-avalanche shortly before Christmas, the mountain village of **Snowbound**, by Bill Pronzini (Fawcett, $1.50), is held hostage at gunpoint by three hoods from L.A.

Kubion, Brodie, and Loxner are on the lam; Kubion, leading them, has shot to kill when their grand-scale heist went sour; the charge, if law closes in, will be Murder One. It's Kubion's idea to rip off the whole valley, draining it of every penny. Though Brodie and Loxner are criminals, they are neither vicious nor psychotic. Kubion is both; if the others allowed it, he would slaughter everyone and leave no witnesses.

Thus begins the battle, with Kubion for hell's side; and, for the village, such likable souls as old Lew Coopersmith, young Johnny Tribucci, or the mysterious newcomer called Zachary Cain. The theme may not be new, but Mr. Pronzini holds us hypnotized until the Christmas Eve climax outside Happy Valley's beleaguered church. **Snowbound** is an action thriller very much for your list.

With Donald Hamilton's **The Terminators** (Fawcett, $1.25) we are swept along through the sixteenth mission in which Matt Helm, secret agent of some unnamed hush-hush bureau, acts as licensed assassin and charms yet another young lady as alluring as she is broad-minded.

At Bergen in Norway Matt meets the female agent with whom he must cruise by steamer up the Norwegian coast. He has no chance to charm this girl, who is murdered almost at once. Matt's quest then becomes a bitter undercover struggle for coastal oil rights. Assisted by an American Navy official, he meets (or collides with) such types as Teutonic Dr. Elfenbein and daughter, beautiful Diana Lawrence, and treacherous Paul Denison amid thunderous action before the unexpected end. Strong recommendation may be taken for granted; neither Matt Helm nor Donald Hamilton will ever let you down.
