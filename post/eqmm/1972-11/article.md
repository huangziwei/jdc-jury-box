## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Yes, something has been achieved. Our dateline is November, and we are within one month of summing up at the end, before lack of worthy new detection compels a choice among masterpieces from the past. Though it will be buttressed by three new adventure-thrillers this masterpiece first saw the light more than fifty years ago. Let's salute the short-story wizardry of Melville Davisson Post, lawyer-philosopher-criminologist, whose death in 1930 ended a writing career both brilliant and popularly successful.

Before the turn of the century Post had invented his Messianic lawyer, Randolph Mason. Ah, these Masons! But his best-known book, **Uncle Abner, Master of Mysteries**, was published by Appleton in 1918. Against a background of the Virginia which became West Virginia during the Great Conflict, it features Uncle Abner, that stern mountain man, in eighteen detective tales as terse as they are powerfully written, each with a whiplash end.

Uncle Abner himself inspires little affection. Always heavy, often sanctimonious, he tends to confuse himself with some Old Testament prophet dispensing God's justice. And yet he radiates strength, including the strength of common sense; by sheer bludgeoning attack he joins the immortals.

Supporting thrillers? Beginning with the escape of a black fugitive from New Prison, **The Wilby Conspiracy**, by Peter Driscoll (Lippincott, $6.95), blasts upcountry through South Africa under hard-fisted Afrikaans rule.

Almost at once we focus on big Jim Keogh, Liverpool Irishman whose engineering degree matches a heart in the right place; on Rina, Keogh's svelte mistress, and on Shack Twala, the escaped prisoner, with all three on the run after Keogh has conked some Boer cop with a wine-bottle. Carrying a fortune in diamonds, cynically but unalterably honest, Keogh must restore those diamonds to their rightful owner.

North they race, Cape Town to Johannesburg, then north again for sanctuary, pursued by Rina's ex-husband and by two Dutch security officers like avenging bogles on the trail. It's all a complexity of problems—who *can* be trusted?—until one last burst of gunplay in the hills. *Vivat Hibernia*! Jim Keogh, who has had our sympathy, emerges in triumph. Verdict: first class.

In another part of Africa, until these early 1960's called the Belgian Congo, mystery-adventure sparks **The Lingala Code**, by Warren Kiefer (Random House, $5.95). Ph.D. Michel Vernon, C.I.A. agent at the American Embassy, faces the inexplicable murder of his pal Ted Stearns, who had no enemies but was shot by some unknown hand.

Who killed Colonel Stearns, and why? We meet the beautiful Swedish girl for whom he has neglected his wife; we meet Congolese police; we meet native leaders; we uncover whole nightmares full of motives until Vernon's solution rounds out a superior thriller with overtones of cloak and dagger, too.

Still another superior thriller, also with overtones of espionage, will be found in George Beare's **The Bee Sting Deal** (Houghton Mifflin, $5.95). Used though we are to crooked escapades in high finance, this one rears its trouble-spot bridge in the East, island to Persian shore, on a scale truly gigantic. Operation Bee Sting, with wholesale massacre, can be thwarted only by wholesale adventurer Victor Stallard and Cynthia Godwin, his well-meaning if ill-mannered Girl Friday. Though pitiless Colonel Davies seems several jumps ahead, he can't stay there. At the torpedo tube of a submarine Stallard chases the bad guys' yacht and will blow her up unless she obeys. Once all echoes have faded away, everybody still alive can be happy.

And so we come round in a circle to end, as we began, with Melville Davisson Post. Since I must send you to second-hand bookshops for **Uncle Abner**, no paperback being available, let me add one other suggestion.

In addition to Abner of the mountains, this author at his best created at least four other detectives: *Monsieur Jonquelle, Prefect of Police of Paris*; Sir Henry Marquis, from *The Sleuth of St. James's Square*; Colonel Braxton, from *The Silent Witness*; Captain Walker, from *Walker of the Secret Service*. *Monsieur Jonquelle* contains one all but perfect story, "The Great Cipher."

Should you encounter any of these books in the course of your browsing, seize and hold. Or perhaps some enterprising publisher will reissue all five. We are not given many writers with the many skills of M. D. Post. Let's honor them when and while we can.
