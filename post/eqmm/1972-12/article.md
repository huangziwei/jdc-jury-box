## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Glancing back over what I have written this year, I see that on no less than two occasions there has been time out for tribute. In July we honored my old friend Anthony Berkeley Cox, a grand master largely unrecognized. In September it was Anglo-Irish Sax Rohmer, remembering how tall stood Fu-Manchu amid the bogeymen of yesteryear. Apart from eulogy, and some few odd matters to be rounded out later, how shall we sum up 1972?

It went much as usual: neither feast nor famine, but with worthy items outweighing the inept. Both sides of the Atlantic provided a duo of homicidal ladies triumphant in straight detection.

Our own Emma Lathen, with *The Longer the Thread*, and the lady who writes as “Paul Kruger,” with *The Bronze Claws*, matched Ngaio Marsh’s *Tied Up in Tinsel* and *Season of Snows and Sins* by Patricia Moyes. To the same category belongs Sara Woods’s *And Shame the Devil*, in which Antony Maitland, barrister-detective, faces problems racial as well as criminal against a background of dourest Yorkshire.

Still! We needn’t have any author “match” another; since all these write well and play fair, it’s unnecessary. Good sleuthing, action-spiced, also animates Bill Pronzini’s *The Snatch*, Peter Lovesey’s *The Detective Wore Silk Drawers*, Hugh Pentecost’s *Birthday, Deathday*. But any prize for ingenious mystification must go to *The Japanese Mistress*, by Richard Neely, which, though seeming to hide nothing up its kimono sleeve, can still wallop our wits with a stunning surprise finish.

In the realm of the mystery-adventure-thriller, you are safe either with *The Curtained Sleep*, Archie Roy’s Scottish conflagration, or with *The Procane Chronicle*, Oliver Bleeck’s New York-Washington murder-go-round. Espionage too has been well served by Victor Canning (*Firecrest*), James Mitchell (*A Red File for Callan*), Berkely Mather (*The Terminators*), and Robert MacLeod (*Path of Ghosts*).

Since books of short stories are notoriously difficult to sell, you may be surprised to find three such collections in a twelve-month. And yet the publisher of each had good reason. Soon after the success of his first anthology, *Rivals of Sherlock Holmes*, Sir Hugh Greene followed it with *More Rivals of Sherlock Holmes*, about gumshoes the world over during those early years 1891-1914. *Murder Most Foul*, M.W.A.'s '71 anthology under the editorship of Harold Q. Masur, upheld the high standard that has never failed us. If aristocratic or titled sleuths were listed by alphabet, beginning with Roderick Alleyn and ending with Prince Zaleski, perhaps the most famous would be Dorothy L. Sayers's Peter Death Bredon Wimsey, younger brother of the Duke of Denver. His twenty detective exploits, gathered together in *Lord Peter*, made (and still make) a dazzling landmark for the year.

Factual crime reconstructed? Again we have fared well. *Great Mysteries of History*, by Kenneth B. Platnick, offers sixteen unsolved enigmas, from the death of Amy Robsart in 1560 to such later grotesques as the Abominable Snowman or the Loch Ness Monster. *The Case of Kitty Ogilvie*, by Jean Stubbs, strikes fiery sparks from an eighteenth-century poison drama of ailing husband, errant wife, gay seducer, inevitable end. Jean Plaidy's high-flying *Murder Most Royal*—one victim Queen Anne Boleyn, second wife of King Henry VIII; the other victim Queen Catherine Howard, his fifth wife—starkly reveals the truth about this callous Caesar, whom legend has changed into an almost avuncular figure of fun.

Today, with new books so outrageously priced, let's welcome our current bonanza of paperbacks. Here are ten titles from Dame Agatha Christie, as well as Sax Rohmer's first three novels about untiring Dr. Fu-Manchu. Nero Wolfe, as directed by Mr. Stout, attacks two difficult cases; James Bond takes on organized crookdom rather than espionage. And don't forget G. K. Chesterton's *The Man Who Was Thursday*, either as philosophical fantasy or as rip-roaring cloak-and-dagger with sensational if non-lethal end.

Now for unfinished business. Some time ago, among many fine Sherlockian studies published by Luther Norris, I praised Marvin Kaye's *The Histrionic Holmes*. Several Holmesians asked where they might procure an item unknown to local booksellers. If you are among the curious, write to Mr. Norris at 3844 Watseka Avenue, Culver City, California 90230. You won't regret it; it should prove an open sesame into the enchanted world of Baker Street.
