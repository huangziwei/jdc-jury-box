### Religion in the Desert

Another you must not miss is **And On the Eighth Day**, by Ellery Queen (Random House, $3.95). Rather more than twenty years ago—the date is April 1943—Ellery, driving back from an (abortive) patriotic mission to Hollywood, gets lost in the desert and encounters a lost religious community which might have strayed from Biblical times.

Here, under the guidance of a leader called *The Teacher*, in a land where greed or violence seems unthinkable, both robbery and murder enter to shake the temple. *And On the Eighth Day* is more than a skillful detective novel, reverent and beautifully written; it has something to say; it explores the nature of good as well as the nature of evil. Ellery the author and Ellery the detective have never been better.

Though **The Neon Haystack**, by James Michael Ullman (Simon and Schuster, $3.50) may be a first novel, it reads like the work of an old pro.

In the haystack of the title, an unnamed Midwestern city, Steve Kolchak, an engineer returned from a job in Arabia, institutes what seems a hopeless search for a human needle. Steve’s younger brother, on a business visit to the city, has got out of a taxi on Clay Street—a sinister neighborhood, ruled by the Syndicate, full of hoods, B-girls, and dubious characters in dubious bars—and there Ed Kolchak has vanished off the face of the earth.

The police can’t find him, or say they can’t; Steve’s arrival in quest of his brother stirs all hell flying from its corners. And yet, for all its jet speed, *The Neon Haystack* never becomes irritatingly tough. If an experienced reader can solve the problems for himself, it is because this is an aboveboard detective novel with all the clues fairly displayed. It won the Inner Sanctum Prize Contest for 1963, and deserved to win. Well plotted, well told, highly recommended.

In **The Mirror Crack’d**, by Agatha Christie (Dodd, Mead, $3.75), we are provided with another tantalizing puzzle from the Queen of Crime. Marina Gregg, a film star no longer young but still attractive, buys a country house near the village of St. Mary Mead. And Marina Gregg should have known better. At that same country house, twenty years ago in a previous story by Miss Christie, somebody has dropped a body in the library; besides, at St. Mary Mead lives Miss Jane Marple, the detecting spinster with whose adventures we are familiar. When a guest at a charity reception is given a poisoned cocktail, it seems evident that the poison was meant for Marina Gregg herself.

A fine brouhaha ensues, with suspects by the dozen and suspicious circumstances by the yard. To one reader at least, Miss Marple is no proper substitute for Hercule Poirot; but the redoubtable Jane, though frail and advanced in years, is undeniably sharp; and clues are generously supplied. Once more the author draws a shrewd, accurate picture of life in present-day England among people who are (a) not of the nobility, (b) not particularly angry about anything, or (c) not pushing for room at the top, but simply human beings like ourselves. Everybody except the victim is safe with Agatha Christie.
