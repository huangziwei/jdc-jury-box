With **The Highbinders**, by Oliver Bleeck (Morrow, $5.95), we join Philip St. Ives, American go-between for the return of heisted valuables, in a cops-and-robbers caper through half-world London.

The fabled thirteenth-century sword of France’s Crusader Saint, King Louis IX, has been stolen from Ned and Norbert Nitry, wealthy British con artists more or less retired. St. Ives must ransom this historic treasure through English Eddie Apex, younger con man representing some mysterious thief unknown.

Careful, now! With a hundred thousand pounds as ransom, with death at every corner, St. Ives dodges snares and booby-traps from graveyard to park to suburban shop. Bloody is the trail, bitter grow frustrations, before he finds St. Louis’s sword in a hiding place so ingenious as almost to defy detection, and cars race towards the final shoot-out on Waterloo Bridge. You can trust Ross Thomas, here alias Oliver Bleeck. It’s a rip-roaring story.

The London of R. G. Jeffreys’s **A Wicked Way to Die** (Walker, $5.95) is London in 1800, its turbulent Age of Reason having overtaken the nineteenth century. Bow Street Runner Jeremy Sturrock, who must not be confused with Bow Street’s Blackstone somewhat later, recounts his own exploits unhampered by modesty. He takes charge at once when young Robert Mytton, son of the great banker, is shot to death in an underground room beneath Mr. Sheridan’s Theatre Royal, Drury Lane.

With Sturrock’s foul-mouthed little assistant we move through pub, slum, and palace; we watch a duel in Hyde Park Fields; we visit the original Grub Street as this able if self-satisfied officer untangles the scheming of thieves, cons, and prostitutes.

Though the secret of the locked room can scarcely be called startling or unexpected, we have not seen all the cards in Mr. Jeffreys’s hand. Impatience must be restrained until Sturrock halts his quarry that night on the marshes. There, in the mist by the mill pond, one real surprise explodes amid blazing musketry to mark a satisfactory climax and a fitting end.
