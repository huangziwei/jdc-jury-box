Pinned in a corner by motive, opportunity, every semblance of guilt, Fran seems lost. The barrister originally defending her sees no line of protection; neither do the instructing solicitors.

But Antony Maitland, who can carry sympathy to the point of quixotry in juggling hot potatoes for a distressed client, accepts her innocence though he knows she's been telling lies. Here it's barrister-detective all the way, from clue to clue and a courtroom disclosure wholly credible. Outstanding; don't miss.

A twentieth-century Jack the Ripper plies his knife with full grisliness throughout Philip MacDonald's **Murder Gone Mad** (Avon, 95¢), first published just forty years ago.

Calling himself the Butcher, terrorizing ultra-respectable Holmdale, this maniac can't be nabbed by the deductions of the brilliant amateur, but only by organized police work. Therefore we bypass nerves-on-edge Colonel Gethryn; our protagonist is another old friend, Scotland Yard Superintendent Arnold Pike.

There are the suspects, named and lined up; which can it be? After several slips Pike's last ingenious trap snares the Butcher under circumstances as horrifying as they are dramatically effective. **Murder Gone Mad** has become murder gone sane.

Speaking of classics, it is time to reassess **The 39 Steps**, by John Buchan (Popular Library, 60¢), that early novel of espionage in the apparently settled world of Britain just before it burst into flames with the holocaust of 1914.

Much blood has flowed, many customs have altered, since those far-off days when the heroine of a spy chronicle did not strip off her clothes and jump into bed with the hero ten minutes after she met him. In fact, there is no heroine in the story. Told by Scottish-born Richard Hannay, who has returned to London from South Africa and become an innocent fugitive on the run, its wild doings sweep us across the Scottish moors, into comedy as well as danger, while Hannay tracks down the agents of the Black Stone.

John Buchan afterwards wrote more adventures of Richard Hannay and his friends, creating notable villains too: vicious Colonel von Stumm in *Greenmantle* or godlike Dominic Medina in *The Three Hostages*. But none has greater effect than the man who could hood his eyes like a hawk, and does so here until, in a literal sense, the whistle is blown at last.
