## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

With entire justification Agatha Christie, in private life Mrs. Max Mallowan, has been called the Queen of Crime. I met her many years ago, when I made my home in England, and have been proud to count her a friend ever since. In person she is as likeable, as engaging, as her books are tricky, not to say sneaky, using both adjectives in high praise.

She herself will probably recall a game of Murder, according to new, different, more elaborate rules, played at my London apartment one night toward the end of the 1930's, with J. B. Priestley officiating as detective. The outcome of that game I have forgotten; Mrs. Mallowan's testimony stays vividly in mind. Challenged to account for her whereabouts at the time of the murder, she replied that she had spent the night with the Archbishop of Canterbury—praying.

Though an ecclesiastical alibi might have been more appropriate to Miss Sayers than to the creatrix of Hercule Poirot, I seem to remember she got away with it. Hercule Poirot and his little gray cells, as well as Agatha Christie and *her* little gray sells, have been bamboozling everybody since the appearance of her first novel, *The Mysterious Affair at Styles*, in 1920.

It is fitting, half a century later, that her American publishers, Dodd, Mead & Co., should reissue her works in the uniform Greenway Edition, modestly priced at $3.95 each. A dozen are available as this is written (April), with more to come. Since those now at hand include my own favorite, **Death on the Nile**, I can pay some small tribute to a great craftsman by choosing it as the subject of my lead review this month.

There will always be debate about Agatha Christie's best detective story. Despite claims for *The A.B.C. Murders* or *Cards on the Table*, I still maintain that **Death on the Nile**, originally published in 1937, contains her most effective surprise finale. At once we are caught up by emotion, not only the broken triangle of Jacqueline de Bellefort versus Linnet, and Simon Doyle, but the emotions of other British or American characters thrown together in conflict as the Nile steamer *Karnak* leaves Assuan on its voyage of bloodshed and panic. Though our enchantress gives us all the facts, hers is a siren voice which seems to be saying one thing while carefully saying something else. Even though you are adept at outguessing authors, be careful!

As there will always be debate about her best novel, so there will always be debate about her most famous. Is *Roger Ackroyd* fair, or does it violate the first rule of the game? While refusing to join in the argument, old devotees will remember that Mrs. Mallowan herself, several years before *Ackroyd*, anticipated her own device with a variation of the same theme which is still more ingenious, entirely fair, and may startle readers as late as 1970. I give no title; I name no names; I simply state the fact.

“Since we know the secret of *Roger Ackroyd*,” you may exclaim, “how can any variation of that theme have the least degree of novelty? Unless she fastens the crime on Mr. Dodd or Mr. Mead, perhaps even on Mr. Cerf of Random House or Miss Kahn of Harper, what in Satan’s name can she possibly do?”

Well, she can; she has done it. You are safe with any Christie novel, old or new. For the Queen of Crime does not age; she remains just as tricky, fully as sneaky, as in that fine unblushing past. I can think of nobody else who has entertained us so consistently, or so well, for a whole half century of homicide.

Of three new novels worth your attention this time round, it is not easy to classify **The Women of Peasenhall**, by R. J. White (Harper & Row, $4.95). Reconstructing a famous (still unsolved) tragedy of rural Suffolk in 1902, the author deals with real persons under their real names, stumbling down dark roads to a doubtful end.

Rose Harsent, the philandering servant girl, did in fact have her throat cut that night at Providence House. William Gardiner, sanctimonious carpenter and Sunday School superintendent, twice stood on trial for murder, free at last because both juries disagreed. Though probably the philanderer they said he was, Gardiner is unlikely to have committed that murder. Using as little fiction as possible, Mr. White attempts almost classic detection and finds a new answer.

It is not without errors. No sophisticated character in 1902 would have talked of Henry Irving doing *The Bells* at Drury Lane; it is as though a sophisticated character of later date referred to John Barrymore playing *Hamlet* at Minsky’s. And some of the slang is modern rather than turn-of-the-century. But the author catches every furtive aspect of village life below the surface. At one time they really did carry on as they carry on here; in all likelihood, God help 'em, they still do.

**Queen's Pawn**, by Victor Canning (Morrow, $5.95) gives us mystery-adventure with speed, power, and impact. Andrew Raikes, our protagonist, is a gentlemanly crook who in most ways does happen to be a gentleman. About to retire before his fortieth birthday with a fortune from many con games, Raikes finds himself caught by the vicious, dwarfish, soft-voiced John Sarling. Just one more operation, with Sarling to direct; *then* he may retire to Devon and Mary Warburton.

Of course Raikes knows it's impossible. In the grip of a leech who can destroy him, he will never escape—unless, with the aid of a dependable old associate and of auburn-haired Belle Vickers, he plots in his own turn to crush the leech forever.

It can be done and is done, with ugliness but expertise. Though one menace has gone, another and worse menace forces him along lines determined from the first: somebody's move against the great new Cunarder, *Queen Elizabeth 2*. Mr. Canning, specialist in shocks, reserves one last shock for the closing pages. And Raikes, doomed soul or not, holds our sympathy until the end.

Combining action; espionage; and all sorts of dirty work behind the scenes, Richard Meade's **The Lost Fraulein** (Random House, $4.95) fully lives up to the high standard he set with *The Danube Runs Red*. Again we meet John Allison, American arms merchant with much experience in using the commodity he sells. Allison needs his combat knowledge from the first chapter, when they try to blast him on a country road in Texas, to the climax in Acanara, West Africa, and that spectacular battle piece on the plain.

With only limited hope of a market, he has bought twenty obsolete Sherman tanks the Pentagon no longer needs. Those tanks, obviously, will turn up where they shouldn't. Pressured by the Great White Chiefs of the State Department to join the Great Black Chiefs of Acanara during civil war, Allison has been driven into an impossible corner. And through everything wanders the lost fraulein herself: Erika Wolf, German sex-bomb, with her amatory talent and her strangely guilty conscience. You won't regret her presence in this superb thriller.
