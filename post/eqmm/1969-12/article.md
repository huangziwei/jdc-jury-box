## Best Mysteries of the Month

recommended by John Dickson Carr

*Salve tibi!* Having now occupied this rostrum for a full year, your mentor feels impelled to utter a few thoughts it has inspired. Before assuming these robes I had sometimes regarded critics as a sour, dyspeptic race apart, And yet here I am one of them, frequently griping away like the worst.

A year ago I discussed reviewing with the editor-in-chief of this magazine.

“Every column,” the editor suggested, “might be a little sermon on mysteries or mystery writing. And you needn’t confine yourself to the current crop. When nothing new seems praiseworthy, feel free to talk about some masterpiece of the past.”

“Excellent!” said I, sounding rather like John H. Watson. “If the going gets really rough, there’s always *The Moonstone* or *The Complete Sherlock Holmes*.”

I was far from serious in this pronouncement; the editor did not call it elementary. But what we considered a fairly remote prospect in sober truth came to pass. Last month, desperate, I found myself reviewing both those works in the same issue.

My critical brethren usually wind up December by selecting what they are pleased to call the year’s ten best. This department, with sound reason, must refrain from any choices arbitrarily so stamped. I can’t be sure publishers have sent me even half the good new mysteries, though virtually certain I have been stuck with all the bad ones.

On the other hand, never think that Rhadamanthus has spent the whole year in a torture chamber. As here already noted, there have been admirable examples of the mystery-adventure and the spy thriller. Mystery-adventures and spy thrillers need display neither that dazzling ingenuity nor that strict fair play which characterize fine detective fiction, although your outstanding item in each category, as with Stanley Ellin’s *The Valentine Estate* or Richard Meade’s *The Danube Runs Red*, may be both highly ingenious and strictly fair.

But what of my *beau idéal* and ewe lamb, the Honest Injun detective novel with full-scale evidence and loaded ending? In that field more than a few established craftsmen—Ellery Queen, Michael Innes, Judson Philips, Ed McBain, Robert L. Fish, Robert Van Gulik—have upheld their reputations well. Even so, this is not quite what you were promised.

Two months ago I said that at the year's end I should be able to cite four brilliant new detective writers who, if not all tyros in the strictest sense, were at least comparative beginners at their craft. And that promise I am proud to keep. A little reflection in fact, can provide five names instead of four. Each has mastered good English. Though one or two have flirted with espionage, each employs Honest Injun technique. You should be safe with anything they write.

High honors, then, for Emma Lathen (*Come to Dust*), for Patricia Moyes (*Death and the Dutch Uncle*), for Dorothy Dunnett (*The Photogenic Soprano*), for Eilis Dillon (*Sent to His Account*), for Mildred Davis (*The Third Half*). Did I say five? No, damme! Let's make it half a dozen.

As the only book not hitherto assessed, try **If the Shroud Fits**, by Paul Kruger (Simon & Schuster, $4.95). Follow a bloodstained track of ingenuity that twists through life high to low, with its vicious multiple murderer always one dodge ahead until young Lawyer Kramer interprets the clues. And to the company of the above five practitioners admit Paul Kruger.

Patricia Moyes, Dorothy Dunnett, Eilis Dillon, Emma Lathen, Mildred Davis, Paul Kruger: two British, one Irish, three American. With the apparent exception of the last-named, they are all women. But the exception is apparent rather than real. My secret agents have learned that 'Mr.' Kruger also buttons 'his' coat on the left-hand side: a disclosure made with full permission of the author and her publisher.

Do I seem to be saying that, among new mystery mongers who play fair, only the ladies can be gentlemen? Oh, no. We could just as well honor the team of Maj Sjowall and Per Wahloo, fifty per cent masculine, with *The Man Who Went Up in Smoke*. Jeremy Potter's *The Dance of Death* is a sound first effort, indisputably male. Nor must we forget E. Richard Johnson (*Mongo's Back in Town*) or Selwyn Jepson (*The Angry Millionaire*). And burn my britches if that's not ten in all!

So there was no real reason for such incessant squawking. The present is bright; with beaming face I anticipate a still brighter future; let me be known as Carr the Glad Guy, blood kin to Pollyanna herself. Meanwhile, as sanity insurance for 1970, has anybody got a copy of *The Complete Father Brown*?
