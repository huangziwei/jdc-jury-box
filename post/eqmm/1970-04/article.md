## Best Mysteries of the Month

recommended by John Dickson Carr

Once more, mysteriously, the prayed-for has happened, with first-class new books in plenty. We are to consider two detective novels of classic pattern, one sensational mystery-adventure; and one how-can-he-escape dilemma of the sort which might entrap any decent man in this mixed-up society. Next month I shall have news which I hope you will find good news; meanwhile, let me stop pontificating and get down to business.

**Murder to Go**, by Emma Lathen (Simon and Schuster, $4.95), as always in this author's work, combines a sound, ingenious detective problem with social comment always witty, sometimes scathing. The Sloan Guaranty Trust, of which our old friend John Putnam Thatcher is senior vice-president, has put up twelve million dollars to back Frank Hedstorm's chain of take-out restaurants, Chicken Tonight. When some unknown hand introduces poison into their food, the plight of Chicken Tonight has grown more than embarrassing; our banker-detective must don his deer-stalker hat and mingle in the backstage battles of this warring outfit.

“Chickens?” exclaims much-married Tom Robichaux. “Well, that’s one helluva way to make big money!” It is also one helluva murder plot, amid credible characters at each other’s throats. The poisoner turns strangler; a situation already emotional soars towards hysteria before Thatcher maneuvers the wiliest of murderers into public betrayal. If Chicken Tonight boasts forty-odd ways of preparing its product, there are forty-odd ways in which this admirable story might be praised.

In **The Stately Home Murder**, by Catherine Aird (Crime Club, $4.50), the country seat of the thirteenth Earl of Ornum—complete with gentlemanly librarian, gentlemanly steward, eccentric family, and ultra-respectable servants—has been opened to the public three days weekly at two and six a head.

We know, of course, that any stately home of England will no more lack its new corpse than it will lack its old ghost. Nor are we disappointed; they duly appear. If at times the action seems inspired less by Agatha Christie than by P. G. Wodehouse (Miss Aird's comedy is excellent), don't let this fool you. With two brutal murders, suspicion constantly flickering, and some shrewd police work by Inspector Sloan, the author takes high place among those homicidal ladies as skilled at plotting as they are smooth of prose.

He must be dead and buriable who would not enjoy this one. Inspector Sloan calls it a traditional story, pointing out the traditional end. But it is the right sort of tradition, which the stately Holmes of England himself would thoroughly approve.

**The Spoilers**, by Desmond Bagley (Doubleday, $5.95) must never be confused with a famous old novel of the same title. This present tale, very much of our own time, opens in London and then shifts to the Middle East for as spectacular an adventure saga as you are likely to encounter. When the daughter of Sir Robert Hellier, millionaire film magnate, lies dead from an overdose of heroin in her sleazy lodging-house, her father vows vengeance against those who have driven her there. To Dr. Nicholas Warren, a sympathetic physician with knowledge of addicts' problems and some inside information as well, he offers the resources of his financial empire if Dr. Warren will gather up a team to do battle.

Their purpose? No less ambitious than to smash the whole international ring which has been supplying two continents with drugs. Their picturesque team includes an ex-Army major for hire, an American con man, a former torpedo expert of the Royal Navy, a psychologist, and a newspaper reporter. Moving out to Teheran and Beirut, the team splits into two teams with explosions all the way. As for the deadly characters they meet, Killer Eastman is bad enough, but for sheer ruthlessness he can't match Jeanette Delorme, the sensual, cold-blooded female spider who first cohabits and then devours. Some fine action scenes on the desert lead to a fantastic sea battle in which Warren's team quite literally sinks the enemy. If you enjoy sensational dirty work, you can't do better than **The Spoilers**.

There is too little space left properly to discuss J. E. Brown's **Incident at 125th Street** (Doubleday, $4.95). But you yourself, from no fault of your own, might find yourself in the position of David Martin, who faces prison and the wreck of his life because innate human decency has sent him to the help of an old man being mugged. Martin's last-moment rescue, through the innate human decency of two or three others, makes both for a trenchant comment on New York's *mores* and for a gripping novel you won't be able to put down.
