## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

The month of October suggests Hallowe’en, jack-o’-lanterns, and all other japes of that night when evil spirits walk; it seems particularly fitting to report on crime.

Of four items chosen for your pleasure, the first two are fair-play detective novels, as fast-paced as they are vivid and ingenious; the third sweeps with bloody deeds of espionage-intrigue through a strife-torn Middle East; and the fourth may seem strange until examination shows that it belongs well within our province.

Thus forewarned, let’s go.

At the outset of **The Blue Hammer** by Ross Macdonald (Knopf, $7.95) we can predict that our old friend Lew Archer, Southern California’s most able private investigator, will be hired by a wealthy client to trace somebody or something mysteriously missing. But, since this author writes a true detective story, full of snares and traps, it’s the only predictable gambit.

The painting of some shapely blonde, sold as the work of a once-famous young artist, disappears from the home of millionaire Jack Biemeyer and his dissatisfied wife. Richard Chantry, the painter, himself vanished into nowhere more than two decades ago. This particular picture, if genuine and no relic of the past, proves Chantry very much alive. In quest of the portrait, despite his relations with Betty Jo Siddon, Archer stumbles through a murky realm of murder, of more disappearance, and of old sins with long shadows. Then the climax erupts. If Mr. Macdonald’s double-surprise disclosure lacks the thunderbolt shock he achieved in the single twist of *The Chill*, it’s a strong, intricately plotted mystery which will please any connoisseur. Score another success for the author and for Lew Archer.

Draw what inference you like from the play-on-words title of Douglas Clark’s **Premedicated Murder** (Scribners, $6.95), which features Superintendent George Masters, Scotland Yard, in a domestic murder imbroglio of human passions on the boil.

The case seems straightforward when Masters and his subordinates arrive at that upper-middle-class village near London. Roger Harte—crippled war hero, kindly soul, most popular man thereabouts—has been stricken with poison in the home of Lowther Close’s most unpopular man, abrasive Milton Rencory, whom only Harte himself liked or encouraged. And Rencory, cattle-food manufacturer, had access to the rare poison used.

But why should even Rencory have killed his one friend? Or, since Harte really was well liked, why should anybody have done it?

Superintendent Masters and his men, though they bicker among themselves, make a shrewd team. As living human characters pass before us, from Harte’s loyal widow to the local doctor, the retired Royal Navy captain, the chemist at the lab, Masters’s eye catches floating fragments of evidence like ashes from a certain bonfire; and he swoops in to end this fine first novel by a newcomer who never once cheats with the clues.

Berkely Mather’s **With Extreme Prejudice** (Scribners, $8.95) sees British Agent Peter Feltham committed against his will to an assassin’s job in the Middle East.

Tracking terrorist bandits, who must be “terminated with extreme prejudice,” leads Feltham south to Suez, then over to Cyprus for private killing amid the strife between Greek and Turk.

Helped by an English-educated Turkish girl, he finds answers in the booby-trap on the mainland, when almost everybody has been terminated except himself. Superior cloak-and-dagger; try it.

Do you wonder why **America in 1876: The Way We Were**, written by Lally Weymouth and designed by Milton Glaser (McGraw-Hill, $15.00), should be reviewed in this magazine?

The Centennial Exhibition at Philadelphia marked the apex of a century’s progress in art, science, industry. That same year saw the apex of a century’s progress in crime, dirty work, and kindred achievements of American ingenuity.

Financial scandal in the Grant administration; “Molly Maguires” condemned to hang *en masse*; a presidential race wherein fast jugglery swung the election to Rutherford B. Hayes: graphic, vividly illustrated, here’s the dark side of our centennial year.

Even new fiction kept pace. Though Mark Twain’s *Tom Sawyer* became a classic of boyhood, its plot center turns on the murder by Injun Joe, heralding menace anything but boyish. And, since Tom later acted as detective in still another murder case, with that note we can leave the review column as well as this splendid survey of the past.
