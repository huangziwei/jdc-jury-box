## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

There’s much to discuss. Beginning with the biography of a royal personage whose career so touched our hobby, we go on to three paperback reprints alike only in superior blood and thunder. These four titles embrace so wide a field of sensationalism that it will allow no introductory philosophizing; I must get down to business at once.

Though Antonia Fraser’s **King James VI of Scotland and I of England** (Knopf, $12.95) has been described by its author as a “biographical essay,” her claim is much too modest. Vividly readable, lavishly illustrated, it sums up all late research on that strange son of Scotland’s doomed Queen Mary, James Stuart, who inherited the throne of Great Britain from Elizabeth I in 1603.

Shambling and slobbering, with tongue too large for his mouth, “the wisest fool in Christendom” sometimes seems little more than a semi-comic grotesque, all contradictory traits. He really loved his wife, Anne of Denmark, despite homosexual leapings towards more than one favorite. While admiring austerity, he was both glutton and sot. His immense erudition too often became mere pedantry, nor could he curb his constant extravagance. And yet, though frequently foolish, he was never stupid. He preserved peace for Britain, against all pressures to the contrary; and, directing every move, he sponsored the version of the Bible which forever bears his name.

What’s he doing in a mystery magazine? Despite James’s innocence, four celebrated crimes influenced his life. Who strangled his father, Lord Darnley, on the night Kirk o’ Field was blown up? As King of Scots, James barely escaped harm in the Gowrie conspiracy, another mystery still unsolved.

True, he ran little risk from that abortive Gunpowder business of ’05, and figured only as *amicus curiae* in the poisoning of Sir Thomas Overbury. But, if I can persuade you to read more than Lady Antonia Fraser’s brilliant summary, and investigate such fascinating historical riddles as the murder of Darnley or the Gowrie imbroglio at Perth, then at least I shall have done something towards justifying my life.

In **A Prayer for the Dying**, by Jack Higgins (Fawcett, $1.25), today’s most reliable hand at action thrillers again holds us hypnotized all the way.

Hired to do murder by the gang boss of an unnamed English city, Martin Fallon, cultured Irish adventurer on the run, kills his victim in the cemetery of Holy Name Roman Catholic Church. Despite everything Fallon wins our sympathy. Despite everything he joins forces with Father da Costa, hard-bitten but human scholar-priest, and Father da Costa’s beautiful blind niece.

Blood flows, fire blazes, as these incongruous allies make war against Dandy Jack Meehan, the gang boss, and Meehan’s vicious young brother, with tension mounting to an unforgettable climax on the scaffolding round the church. If there were any grade higher than A plus, it should be given.

At the outset of Dick Francis’s **Blood Sport** (Pocket Books, $1.25) you can predict that “sport” will concern horses and “blood” must be interpreted in a double sense.

But you won’t anticipate what happens. After frustrating an attempted murder on the Thames, British Secret Agent Gene Hawkins must discard cloak and dagger to trace certain champion stallions, each worth a fortune, which have mysteriously vanished in America.

Success is impeded by two helpful women, over-young Lynnie as well as over-tipsy Eunice. With roaring adventure which sweeps his hero from Kentucky to a dude ranch farther west, from Nevada to California and back again, Mr. Francis rounds out this perilous kidnapping saga with all his customary expertise and grace of style. *Anglice*, top drawer.

**American Gothic**, by Robert Bloch (Fawcett, $1.25), centers on a picturesque mass murderer who rears his gimcrack “castle” in the World’s Fair Chicago of 1893.

He’s all charm-over-cruelty, this fellow. We might find him incredible if “G. Gordon Gregg” in the novel did not so resemble an actual contemporary monster—H.H. Holmes, whose real name was Mudgett—even to his alliterative *nom de guerre*.

Gregg might have gone on slaughtering if he had never met Crystal, our girl reporter. Suspense explodes at last when he seems to have her trapped, and he ends in fiery holocaust on the same night as the fair. Grisly but good.
