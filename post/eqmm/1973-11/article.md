## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Though the calendar may show an expiring year, once more I am compelled to write this in midsummer and in a dearth of material. Still! Since your mentor can recommend three new books, backing them up with only one triumphant classic of old, that's not a bad average for any given season.

Our very mixed bag runs thus:

**Blackstone’s Fancy**, by Richard Falkirk (Stein and Day, $6.95), roars with the second adventure of Edmund Blackstone, hardest nail of the Bow Street Runners, in the roaring eighteen twenties under roaring old King George IV. Two months ago I praised Blackstone's first recorded case, when he thwarted a plot to kidnap Princess Victoria, child heir to the throne. Here, since prizefighting is against the law, he must pretend to stamp it out while secretly training his own candidate, Ebony Joe, as an opponent for Houseman, the Bristol Butcher.

Against the world of the bare-knuckle Fancy, recreated in all its color and brutality; parade lords, scullions, ladies, harlots, bookies, hangers-on for blood-spattered pugilism which becomes also blood-spattered murder. Our protagonist needs not only fists or firearms; he needs all his wits to detect the murderer. At the end of the decade, when Peel's plans mature, surely they will make him Mr. Superintendent Blackstone, Metropolitan Police? He's too strong a character for limbo; he must not fade or disappear.

When the call-girl is found messily murdered and headless at a midtown hotel in Lillian O'Donnell's **Don’t Wear Your Wedding Ring** (Putnam, $4.95), we join the quest for her killer with Detective Third Grade Norah Mulcahaney, attractive Manhattan policewoman, and doughty Sergeant Joe Capretto. But the call-girl, it develops, was a well-to-do young married woman of good social background.

Quite a few affluent young matrons, as revealed by a second-if-less gruesome murder, have joined a part-time prostitution ring—run by whom? And why violent death as the end?

With new twists at every turn, always on an edge of nerves, Norah Mulcahaney and Sergeant Joe follow that wicked trail from Lark Enterprises to the airport motel, from victim Harriet Weitz to near-victim Carla Jarks. It seems hopeless; their best efforts achieve nothing but a disastrous bomb explosion until they gain the authorities' consent to Nora's daring ruse.

Detective Mulcahaney, apparently disgraced and dismissed, will pose as a call-girl recruit. To trap the ring-boss she risks her life and almost loses it at the sensational finale of police procedure admirably managed by a woman writer who knows the job.

In **The 92nd Tiger**, by Michael Gilbert (Harper & Row, $6.95), ever-reliable Mr. Gilbert turns from superior detection to a fast-paced adventure thriller vibrant with satiric comedy.

Star of British television as an all-conquering secret agent called the Tiger, for ninety-one triumphant episodes Hugo Greest has polished off the villain. Now that they're cancelling the series—despite immense popularity Hugo is almost forty—what next? There can't be any such role as the Tiger in real life?

But there can. Directed by the Foreign Office, accepting urgent invitation, our erstwhile Tiger becomes (of all things) military adviser to Sheik Ahmed of Umran in the Middle East.

And we believe it. Neither super-hero nor inept bungler, the real Hugo is intelligent, imaginative, and with a sense of humor; he captures our sympathies at once. Backed by the sheik, by the sheik's young son and by Tammy, the American heroine; Hugo coils his way through undercover dirty work to its eruption in outright war. By keeping both his head and his sense of humor, a true Briton, he emerges victorious, wins the girl, and lands an even better offer from the lords of television. Don't miss this one.

So far as I know, Richard Harding Davis's sensational masterpiece, **In the Fog** (1901), was the sole venture into mystery by that once-celebrated scribe. Though short enough to be included in a Modern Library anthology almost fifty years ago, it was published on its own by R. H. Russell, one of the illustrators being Frederic Dorr Steele, who immortalized William Gillette as Sherlock Holmes; and you may still find a copy at your friendly secondhand bookshop.

At a small and select London club, including the young American who calls himself Lieutenant Sears, each of various members adds his contribution to a tale of double murder in peasouper fog, building tension towards a totally unexpected double-twist end. "Davis, thou shouldst be living at this hour; Davis, thou wert well ahead of thy time."
