From the dark shadows of **Postscript to a Dead Letter**, by Donald MacKenzie (Houghton Mifflin, $5.95), France’s *Union Corse*, the criminal brotherhood like one on our own shores, stretches tentacles across Paris to crush and destroy.

Radnor Brown, hapless young American, has stumbled on evidence of the brotherhood’s traffic in heroin; it lands him only in jail on a false charge. Breaking out to join Phoebe, his attractive sister, young Brown finds a swashbuckling champion in Ross Macintyre, Canadian reporter for the London *Post*.

Aided by George Gunn, an old pal; by Ilinka Ostravo, that surprising lady from Russia; and by Misty Farrell, the reporter’s own Girl Friday, Ross Macintyre sets out to prove his belief through adventures loaded with high explosive at every step.

Our Canadian Scot won’t be beaten. Past booby-traps set by cops, robbers, robber cops, and robbers in government, Macintyre batters his way to the confrontation at, of all places, a convent with a very strange Reverend Mother. And Donald Mackenzie, himself a Canadian Scot, has added another brilliant mystery-adventure to his already impressive list.

In a stuffily curtained mansion of London, 1890, Jean Stubbs’s monumental **Dear Laura** (Stein and Day, $6.95) erupts with bitter domestic clashes leading to death by poison. Though there seem to be familiar elements—Laura, the beautiful young wife; Theodore, the cold and too-reserved husband; Titus, the dashing younger brother—yet no event here follows any familiar pattern; we may expect only the unexpected.

When investigation proves Theodore’s death to have been suicide and not murder, can any author sustain a fever of interest for almost three hundred pages? And yet Miss Stubbs does just that. Surprise piles on surprise until, with one last twist of the knife, we are left with a cumulation which must be called satisfactory because it is so obviously inevitable.
