## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

*Selah*! Even as your mentor climbs up to his rostrum for the reading of the lesson, permit me to announce at once that there will be corn in Egypt, good times a-coming, almost an *embarras de richesse* of the sensational. Beginning with detection extraordinary from two homicidal ladies at their slippery best, we proceed through detection in a different mood to mystery-adventure-espionage all the more deadly because every step of that narrow area has been mined or booby-trapped by the wrong enemy.

The dazzling display called **Murder-Go-Round** by Agatha Christie (Dodd, Mead, $7.95) offers *Thirteen at Dinner*, *The A.B.C. Murders*, and *Funerals Are Fatal*, three cases for Hercule Poirot spanning his creatrix's devious ways through two golden decades, '33 to '53.

*Thirteen at Dinner*, which years ago in England I read under the title of *Lord Edgware Dies*, piles illusion upon illusion when that finicky peer is stabbed under circumstances apparently damning to his light-headed wife. *The A.B.C. Murders*, by many considered Dame Agatha's masterpiece, was discussed in this column only a few months ago.

And that's not all. *Funerals Are Fatal*—through some oversight I missed it until now—will wallop you with a true Christie thunderbolt in the penultimate chapter. To this novel of rural dirty work, in my view, belongs the prize. Only a connoisseur will spot the murderer, and even he may choose the right culprit on the wrong evidence. It happened to me, I must admit; for which of us, in any fair contest, can outwit the Queen of Crime?

When first you glance at it, many elements of Ngaio Marsh's **Tied Up in Tinsel** (Little, Brown, $6.95) may seem all too familiar. At Halberds Manor, the luxurious country house of improbably named Hilary Bill-Tasman, that compulsive talker who has inherited his fortune from dubious sources, young Mr. Bill-Tasman is entertaining a Christmas party. We have the ailing uncle, the sharp-tongued aunt, the beautiful *fiancee*, the urbane old antique-dealer with the Cockney accent. We have the eccentric servants, each a proved ex-murderer. When somebody dresses up as an ancient Druid, bewigged and bewhiskered, no doubt death will claim its victim.

Sheer narrative skill touches familiar materials with novelty, even freshness. Ngaio Marsh, like Agatha Christie now a Dame Commander of the British Empire, gives us real people in a storm of emotion we can understand. And Dame Ngaio, no less wily than Dame Agatha herself, has devised a plot honorably fair but of double-distilled misdirection to bamboozle the unwary: strong entertainment for any reader.

In **Birthday, Deathday** by Hugh Pentecost (Dodd, Mead, $4.95), New York's super-swank Hotel Beaumont again provides a stage for bloody deeds and for the detective talent of Pierre Chambrun, its manager. Brutal, pitiless General Ho Chang will pay a visit this Eastern potentate may not survive. Neil Drury, whose family the general slaughtered, has vowed vengeance and disappeared.

But we can't hope for the success of a would-be murderer; or can we? Where might Neil Drury be hiding? And, in any case, how to crack that impenetrable ring of guards? Mr. Pentecost, one of our own most experienced bafflers, keeps tension at fever pitch as other crimes build towards confrontation and the eye of ultimate truth. Continue; have no fear. The finale won't let you down.

Since a secret agent in fiction so often must battle his own colleagues before meeting any alien foe, you will not be surprised that the whole action of Victor Canning's **Firecrest** (Morrow, $5.95) explodes inside that British hush-hushery called the Department, its favored weapon deceit and its cheapest commodity human life.

Professor Dilling, inspired both by genius and by his lissom mistress, Lily, has invented a secret weapon. Suspecting dirty work, he has hidden the plans of “Firecrest” before a heart attack strikes him dead. John Grimster, the Department’s best operative, must take over Lily and find those missing plans.

Then, of course, they will eliminate Lily; they may eliminate their own agent too. A savage tug-of-war, Grimster against authority, wrenches muscles from London to the country, from Lily’s bedroom to the lion-enclosure at Woburn. The final smash may be unjust; it may be unpleasant; given such premises, it is merely inevitable.
