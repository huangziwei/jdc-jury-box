“It can’t be stolen,” the authorities say. But it can be; it almost is.

Mastermind Stephenson, employing musclemen as ruthless as himself, kidnaps an all-too-human young policewoman for the plotters’ blackmail weapon against Detective Chief Superintendent Roger West and all-too-human Sergeant Tom Batten, the policewoman’s lover.

It’s a near thing, but don’t worry. Before they finish, with Salisbury Cathedral all but ripped apart in flames, Superintendent West has met his formidable challenge and John Creasey has written his best story.

As an explosive thriller which gives no respite, Robert Ludlum’s **The Matlock Paper** (Dial Press, $7.95) will blow your ears off. The grimy silver-paper message found on a dead man in Turkey has given Washington’s Department of Justice their clue to the vast secret criminal organization called Nimrod. Nimrod’s center, they have good reason for believing, may be found at sedate Carlyle University in Connecticut. And so maverick-spirited young Dr. James Matlock, Associate Professor of English, is enlisted to learn Nimrod’s secrets and destroy it.

No sooner has our young academic been briefed than some unknown hand guns down the Justice Department’s agent in a phone booth. Matlock, with a beautiful mistress on campus and no caution to speak of, plunges headlong through a nightmare world with menace round every corner and behind every bush. As a connoisseur of mysteries you will probably spot the principal villain; but, torture or tragedy notwithstanding, you won’t stop reading until the end.

Very substantially paperbound, **Tales of Terror and the Supernatural**, by Wilkie Collins, selected and introduced by Herbert van Thal (Dover, $3.00), well illustrates, as though we hadn’t known it already, that this Victorian master knew a thing or two about his craft.

These tales, gleaned from *After Dark* (1856) and *The Queen of Hearts* (1859), deal very little with the supernatural; in most cases ghostly tremors are invoked only to be dispelled. Mania or human evil provides the *cauld grue*, from Latin vengeance in “Blow Up with the Brig!” to the ingenious devilishness of “A Terribly Strange Bed.” Take particular note of a fine detective short story, “The Biter Bit,” written long before our first fair-play detective novel, *The Moonstone*. Be pleased to try it, good brethren; with Wilkie Collins you can’t go wrong.
