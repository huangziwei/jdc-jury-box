## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

As I prepare this November column at the beginning of August, the pressure of heat outside an air-conditioned study seems to invalidate any suggestion that 1970 can be nearing its close. And yet such is the case. When I became *EQMM*’s critic almost two years ago, more than one friend prophesied doom.

“You’ll be a target,” I was warned, “for half the letter-writing cranks in this country. They’ll bawl you out, scream at you, tell you your judgments are crazy or crooked or both.”

Well, that prophecy failed to materialize. On the contrary, all letters here received have been literate, intelligent, and perceptive; they were much appreciated. But it does bring to mind a long-past incident not without overtones of comedy.

The most explosive book-reviewing uproar of my experience occurred in London towards the end of the nineteen thirties, and involved a left-wing sage who each week pontificated for the *Evening Standard*. Though it’s to be hoped that since then he has been eaten by bears, like those children who mocked the prophet, yet the man may still be alive; not to rouse old wars, let’s give him the pseudonym of Richmond Fall.

Trouble began with his notice of a detective novel, *Hercule Poirot’s Christmas*, by Agatha Christie. Not content merely with damning *Hercule Poirot’s Christmas*, the pundit disclosed (1) the identity of the murderer, (2) the murderer’s motive, (3) nearly every detail of the trick by which the crime had been committed, (4) how the detective knew it.

Repercussions soon followed. The next day, foaming with rage, Anthony Berkeley phoned me to ask questions. Had I seen this outrage in the *Standard*? As honorary secretary of the Detection Club, would I take it lying down? Write to the newspaper; answer that so and so at once!

It was done. In restrained terms I pointed out that, while a critic must say what he likes about the merits of any book, it’s not quite the clean potato carefully to give away secrets on whose effect its author so much depends.

My protest triggered a real blast from Richmond Fall. Our pundit, thoroughly roused, dealt with the challenge by ignoring it and attacking along another line. Though he knew the rules, he said, this trash called detective fiction was a nuisance, a bore, an affront to readers’ intelligence; he’d do anything to swat such rubbish.

Your correspondent could retire; others entered the fray for what became an embittered slanging-match between those who liked detective fiction and those who hated it. Despite a timely contribution from Dorothy Sayers, reminding everybody that the point at issue was not the worth of mysteries but how they should be reviewed, most combatants on both sides also forgot the point.

After all, there is a historic parallel. That row suggests the occasion, an eighteenth-century literary event afterwards described by Sir Walter Scott, when Dr. Samuel Johnson met the Scottish economist, Adam Smith. Dr. Johnson called Adam Smith a liar; Adam Smith called Dr. Johnson a son of a bitch. This, so far as we know, was the only recorded exchange between the great lexicographer and the author of *The Wealth of Nations*.

Descending to no opprobrious epithets, scattering flowers all the way, I can report on four straight detective stories, alternately British and American, each of sound construction and entire fair play. The first three are new; the fourth, published a few years ago, has now reappeared in paperback.

In **Wobble to Death**, by Peter Lovesey (Dodd, Mead, $4.50), the foggy gaslight of 1879 illumines one of those brutal Victorian sporting events, the six-day indoor walking race or ‘wobble,’ in which athletes achieved miracles of distance and punishment, even though they trained on booze. It also illumines murder. At Islington Agricultural Hall a dozen hopefuls take the field, led by the gentleman champion, Captain Chadwick, and his not so gentlemanly rival, Charlie Darrell. One contestant falls dead of poison on the second day; there will be another victim as the cruel pace goes on.

Here are true Victorians, not pious frauds of legend: Herriott and Jacobson, respectively promoter and manager; Mostyn-Smith, that strange-looking amateur of medicine; Monk, Charlie Darrell’s trainer; Zora, Charlie’s seductive wife. Sound investigation by Sergeant Cribb rounds out a first-rate story of sustained thrill.

**In the Death of a Man**, by Lesley Egan (Harper & Row, $4.95), offers much confusion to Jesse Falkenstein, the lawyer who says he won’t touch criminal cases, to Sergeant Andrew Clock of Los Angeles Homicide, and to fat old Edgar Walters, that perceptive armchair sleuth.

Lynette Lester, daughter of millionaire, rough-diamond Pat Gannon and friend of Falkenstein’s sister, has consulted the lawyer about her marital troubles with enigmatic Glen Lester. Trouble deepens when Lynette’s husband is found shot to death, apparently outside her own men’s office building. Who killed Glen Lester is not the most difficult problem three investigators have to solve. They must trace somebody backwards along a very twisted trail; and, as usual in novels where several ‘unrelated’ cases flicker in the background, one unrelated case will bear watching.

There are times when Miss Egan can be as ingeniously sneaky as Agatha Christie herself. With the text of her teletype message she slips in a central clue *almost* unnoticed. That’s the stuff: good hunting all the way to a wry, ironic end.

Some may object to Douglas Clark’s **Deadly Pattern** (Stein & Day, $4.95) as being beef-and-ale British detection of the stodgily familiar sort. You may not quite believe in the decaying East Coast town with its five buried corpses. You may not be over-fond of Chief Inspector Masters or grumpy Inspector Green.

But it’s honest detection, for which we should be grateful. Its mosaic of evidence has been made as intricate as complete. And the capture of a monomaniac killer resolves every doubt.

Last on our list, though far from last in order of merit, we have **Accounting for Murder**, by Emma Lathen (Avon, 95¢), an early effort of perhaps the most distinguished talent to appear since 1960. Featuring Banker-Detective John Putnam Thatcher, senior vice-president of New York’s Sloan Guaranty Trust, Miss Lathen has given us another of those ingenious exercises in crime-*cum*-big business which are really exercises in crime-*cum*-human nature. That something must be very wrong at the firm called National Calculating raises hell on Wall Street even before an accountant probing their affairs is strangled with the cord of his own adding-machine. It makes us look steadily in the wrong direction.

Apart from all her skill at plotting, the author’s great skill lies in her ability to find real people inside official masks and stuffed shirts. These bankers, these company directors, these whiz kids of high finance—they’re not godlike souls, praise be; they’re as human as you and I.
