## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

And now, past the year’s middle, we encounter three thrillers—the first of them a near-detective novel—which rank as sound and praiseworthy rather than distinguished, although they are backed up by one really notable achievement, a vast and erudite compendium of information about your favorite reading matter. If I can’t cheer for any except the last-named, with entire justice I can commend. There’s no occasion to gripe.

**Legend in Green Velvet**, by Elizabeth Peters (Dodd, Mead, $7.95), carries us to Scotland with our own attractive Susan, romantic-minded American girl who has been hired to assist a famous Scots archaeologist in his quest after ancient secrets.

Even before leaving Edinburgh for Professor Campbell’s dig, Susan is swept into a whirl of sensationalism, stumbles across a murder, and among other enigmatic characters meets a young laird of more than picturesque heritage.

Vivid background as well as authentic history animate rapid-paced scenes which almost become detection. Though the author might have given us more clues to her master criminal, on page 221 she explodes a genuine surprise. Thenceforward, despite so many stabs of danger, the saga of Susan and her young laird whirls on to its satisfactorily happy end.

Set in near-future France under flamboyant President Florian, Colin Forbes’s **The Stone Leopard** (Dutton, $7.95) erupts at once with violence, treachery, and intrigue.

An attempt to assassinate President Florian before leaving for his Moscow conference is frustrated by Police Prefect Marc Grelle, who shoots down the would-be killer but provokes only a storm of perplexities. “The Leopard,” legendary Resistance hero of identity almost unknown, was also a Communist who would destroy all those opposing Russia. Though supposed dead for years, this dangerous enemy may secretly have become some high official in the French government.

Into the murderous game are drawn David Nash of America, Alan Lennox of Britain, and Colonel René Lasalle, former French Intelligence officer now exiled by President Florian. Since Moscow has sent a team of assassins to liquidate the only three witnesses who can identify Monsieur Leopard, we watch the murderers nailed one by one as they almost accomplish their mission.

That’s not the end of uproar. The tale sweeps to its spectacular climax at Charles de Gaulle Airport, where Monsieur Leopard gets his deserts, ironically, from a weapon supplied for other purposes by the Soviet Union itself; and one more French crisis has forever passed into history.

Again narrated by British Secret Agent Philis of SR(2), Ritchie Perry’s **Your Money and Your Wife** (Houghton Mifflin, $6.95) finds Philis enraged when his Norwegian honeymoon is interrupted by the kidnaping of the bride. And again it’s Ubiquitous Plotter Petrov, K.G.B. To rescue his wife Philis, first name never used, must appear publicly as defector, traitor, and murderer.

All these he seems to be. His bondage whisks him from a remote cottage in Scotland, where Soviet vengeance executes one of their own defectors, to the East German prison officially called an insane asylum.

Again appearances are deceptive. Up their sleeves both Philis and Department SR(2) have been keeping cards concealed; only when our hero escapes from prison, taking with him another captive Briton, do we appreciate their play. And in London there’s a final bloody exchange to settle the score at last.

With **Encyclopedia of Mystery and Detection**, by Chris Steinbrunner and Otto Penzler (McGraw-Hill, $19.95), my two colleagues across the alley have compiled a treasure house for casual readers as well as fans, and supplied the answer to almost any question.

Smooth, exhaustive, but always entertaining, it covers plays, films, radio, and television in addition to novels and short stories.

If you have a favorite author or sleuth among the elect, you will find biographies of both. Lesser names have not been neglected, nor such writers from the long past as Anna Katharine Green, Jacques Futrelle, T.W. Hanshew.

One solemn note concerning the London Detection Club. Of some thirty original members when they elected me in 1936, only two of us now remain alive.

But solemnity must not mitigate praise. This encyclopedia by my colleagues and their associates should become *the* definitive work for our time.
