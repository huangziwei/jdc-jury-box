## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Two detective novels, one spy story, one cops-and-robbers chronicle landing its sneak punch before the bell. That’s the tally; let your mentor begin with yet another personal confession.

When lack of worthy new books compels me to recommend an old one, I don’t mind. Conscience will squirm, hoot, or gibber only when the old book in question is not readily available. With ten more Agatha Christie titles now issued in paperback, conscience can remain quiet. And there has been no evasive waffling about “suspense.” Apart from some few novels without famous sleuth, each Poirot or Miss Marple adventure is honorably called a murder mystery, as it ought to be.

Of Hercule Poirot, “I’m sick of the little blighter!” his *creatrix* remarked one night at dinner. It startles me to recall that she said this fully thirty-five years ago. No doubt she has since become reconciled; we others have liked and admired the Belgian with the moustaches since his first appearance in 1920.

Among ten titles reissued, Dame Agatha’s **Third Girl** (Pocket Books, 95¢) claims pride of place because it must rank with the best. Norma Restarick, who can’t be sure whether or not she committed a murder, first calls on Poirot and then bolts away into hiding. Does the secret concern Norma’s wealthy father, her new stepmother, old Sir Roderick Horsefield, or the loutish young artist she may marry? What of the two girls with whom she shares a flat, or of one victim fallen to death from the seventh floor? Aided by Mrs. Ariadne Oliver, that eminent writer, Poirot sifts clues in yet another victory for the little gray cells.

Don’t be alarmed at the heavily academic atmosphere of **The Serpent Under It**, by Edith Taylor (Norton, $5.95). This viper (see Shakespeare, as also for the names of the cats) menaces Hoyt College when Anne Redmond, wife of an English instructor, finds Professor Archibald stabbed in that sunlit garden of peace.
