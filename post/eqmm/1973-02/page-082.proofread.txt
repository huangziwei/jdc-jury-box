## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Once more, let it be said, I feel a certain disquiet. Of four new books to be recommended this month, three have been listed by the publishers as “novels of suspense,” the fourth escaping that designation because it deals factually with one aspect of sensationalism in fiction. But those first three: “novels of suspense,” are they? Sometimes I wonder if we shall ever be free of this dreary term.

No, it’s not the first time I have been moved to remonstrance. Some weeks ago a New England correspondent asked questions.

“Frankly,” he wrote, “what’s wrong? Why your gripe against *calling* ‘em novels of suspense? Can you give one good reason?”

Yes, if you care to listen. I say nothing of the fact that this term is the lazy man’s way of ticketing every kind of blood and thunder, even when it contains about as much suspense as one of Aesop’s fables. The real reason lies deeper.

Though any good mystery must contain the quality of suspense, that quality will be only one element among many. When you call the whole thing “a novel of suspense,” you deliberately ignore the whole for one of its parts. It is as though the romance of Edwin and Angelina, ending in wedding bells, were to be described as “a novel of church history.”

Never mind; let’s proceed with this month’s harvest.

Combining fast action-thriller with mystery against Air Force background, **Cornered at Six**, by Thomas Patrick McMahon and Major Brian Patrick McMahon, U.S.A.F. (Simon and Schuster, $6.95), admirably succeeds at both.

When a commercial airline hostess is messily slain at Floyd Bennett Field in New York, there seems only one possible culprit. Sergeant Willie Joe Kirk, Negro crew chief to General Sam Huston Stanton, that martinet two-star commander, has left incriminating evidence when he fled. General Stanton, politicking hard for a third star, will help his sergeant if it’s convenient; otherwise, of course, Willie Joe must suffer.
