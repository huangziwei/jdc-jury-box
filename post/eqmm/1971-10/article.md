## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Very well; let’s try the experiment.

The first three titles of our four-book agenda for this month are quite safe. Beginning with an admirable new detective novel by one of the great masters, who is also editor-in-chief of this magazine, our second title sweeps us into high-powered European espionage *cum* Satanism with American protagonists. The third novel, adventure-thriller (American hero, British heroine), penetrates very far afield both in motive and in action, though physically it penetrates no deeper than brawling Algeria.

And our fourth title? Ah, that’s it. That’s the experiment.

“Experiment?” you may well burst out in annoyance. “What’s all this about an experiment?”

The question will answer itself as we proceed. In **A Fine and Private Place** by Ellery Queen (World, $5.95) Ellery and his father face a situation which, apparently originating in sheer emotion, becomes a classic murder problem of the grisliest kind.

No modern father, we are told, could sell his daughter into matrimonial slavery according to Victorian custom: not even a father like improvident, aristocratic Wallace Ryerson Whyte, whose beautiful daughter, Virginia, has caught the eye of Billionaire Nino Importuna, three times her age and of no enviable temper.

But Whyte, his crooked ways as natural as his charm, is blackmailed into arranging it. If Virginia remains Nino’s wife for five years, according to the marriage contract, she will inherit everything.

And Nino’s scheme seems successful. Despite a husband with life ruled by his own superstitions, despite the fact that she herself has fallen hard for young Peter Ennis, remain his wife Virginia does until the deadline is past. Nino’s two brothers have already met violent deaths. Then, with that fifth anniversary just attained, Nino himself is dramatically slain as though for the whoop of a cosmic jeer. Enter our son-with-father detective team. This book, with its dazzling display of crooked trails and straight clues, shows yet again how well its author deserves to be called a master.

In David St. John's **The Sorcerers** (Fawcett, 75¢), paperback reprint of some notable espionage first published two years ago, Washington Attorney Peter Ward has been commissioned for undercover work by one of those government undercover agencies designed to end trouble at the start. Though American security forces work with Canadian, there have been ugly moves in Canada; no doubt a leak, with Russia's K.G.B. as active as ever. The daughter of a Canadian government V.I.P. has vanished without a trace from her exclusive school in Switzerland. Peter Ward must find lovely Diane, restore her, and set all things right. Finding Diane, even after a wild chase across Europe, proves less difficult than holding her when she is found. But to Ward the girl is worth this difficulty or any difficulty.

Our dance of espionage, background Paris, becomes a dance of Satanism as lurid as though Old Nick himself led the reel. At the climax we are blown skyward towards an ending as satisfactory to us as to any of the other good guys on our side. For action, my brethren, you can't beat this one.

Though dealing with international shell games as well as with mystery-adventure, **Assignation in Algeria**, by John Lee (Walker, $5.95), may be listed among the latter. You must meet Brian Douglas, American newsman with base in Spain. Meet Zalie, that alluring English girl. Meet Rick Forrester, lanky South African with too-large teeth and too-ready smile. Off on his mission, Douglas must do what he can against thrice-crooked odds.

But never mind. “Courage!” some voice seems to be whispering through the heat of conflict. “There will be other problems to solve, other beaches to lie on, other drinks to taste.” So be it. With the cloak and dagger, fortunately, tomorrow always comes.

That’s three novels dealt with, and no fourth. What do we do? In recommending this anthology of criminous criticism, **The Mystery Writer’s Art**, edited by Francis M. Nevins, Jr. (Bowling Green University Press: paperback, $3.50; hardcover, $8.95), be sure I don’t confuse you with the ‘average’ reader. Were you the average reader, sir or madam, you would not be following this column, perhaps not even this magazine. You have special interest in, almost a passion for, mystery and the ways of mystery. Here between covers, set down by those who know and love their field, you will find research, history, theory’s immense challenge. No vengeful lightning-bolt will strike as you read, but only sweet airs attend.
