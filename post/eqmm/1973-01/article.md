## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Once more, of necessity, January’s introductory assessment will contain a warning that most items here considered must belong to the old year rather than the new. Never mind; it’s a very fair complement of blood and thunder. I can provide one espionage exploit of such superior quality as to deserve pride of place; two detective novels contrasted in their approach; and one collection of classic tales, time *circa* Edwardian, here at last rescued from oblivion. With such strong fare to claim attention until we are properly launched for '73, not a soul among us need complain.

Swift, deadly move or counter-move throughout William P. McGivern’s **Caprifoil** (Dodd, Mead, $6.95) finds American Agent August Spencer and Britain’s likable “Bunny” Ackerley locked in combat against unseen forces, their lives always in hazard with every hand against them.

André Leroi, their former associate and France’s own ace agent, has disappeared with a warning of disaster which may soon explode. Against earth’s security Arab fanatics called the Saracen Vector have devised a plot so wild yet so practical as almost to stagger imagination.

Can Spencer thwart the design? Can anybody thwart it? For in action there’s no pause or break. Not only do we race with Spencer from Paris to Frankfurt and back again, while death ticks like a bomb at his ear. Adroit shifts of narrative carry us from the White House in Washington to the terrorists’ stronghold outside Algiers, where Arab Colonel Gamal awaits triumph with General Mercier, the French traitor who backs him.

But nemesis has been challenged. As the American aircraft carrier approaches Algiers, as the sky thunders to jets or darkens with descending parachutes, one last private battle sprays bullets through the Arabs’ fort to write *finis*. August Spencer survives, taking wing to join his latest mistress in Mexico. And, since Mr. McGivern is a hypnotic story-teller, we have believed every word along the way.

**A Trout in the Milk**, by Michael Underwood (Walker, $5.95), has as background the City of London, that narrow area of London Town itself, and in particular the ancient legal haunt of the Temple.

Eminent Barrister James Geddy has begun the Old Bailey defense of Frank Manion, notorious wrong 'un as yet unconvicted, when a harmless young law student is messily stabbed to death for no apparent reason whatever. Though crime-hardened wrong 'un and innocent student are total strangers, their cases seem strangely related as circumstantial evidence gathers round both.

“Some circumstantial evidence is very strong,” said Thoreau, “as when you find a trout in the milk.” Hence our title; hence the problem attacked by Detective Chief Superintendent Roffey, City Police. The author, Department of Public Prosecutions, portrays every aspect of a barrister’s life: upstairs, downstairs, in and out of chambers. If at times the legal gentry are more compelling than the story, Mr. Underwood points his surprise finish with a clue so obvious that it may slip past unnoticed. Take care!

Despite its mock-whimsical title, which may be disregarded, in Peter Lovesey’s **Abracadaver** (Dodd, Mead, $4.95), an authentic Victorian background again vividly recreates London ninety years ago, with dirty work amid the boozy, brawling uproar of the music hall. What has seemed no more than a plague of brutal jokes freezes suddenly into deliberate murder, providing headaches for doughty Sergeant Cribb of the old, original Scotland Yard.

It’s a tangled business, both evil and intricate. Every police move seems frustrated by almost superhuman cunning until Cribb’s trap nails the murderer and ends a hunt as spectacular as it has been fairly marked with clues. Very much Grade A.

Unless you are overprejudiced against the briefer form, don’t miss **Best Max Carrados Detective Stories**, by Ernest Bramah, selected and introduced by E. F. Bleiler (Dover, $3.00). Winnowed from three all-but-forgotten volumes into durable paperback, here are nine tales of Max Carrados, blind but all-seeing contemporary of Sherlock Holmes.

Though there have been other blind detectives—Damon Gaunt, Thornley Colton, today’s Duncan Maclain—you will find Carrados impressive as he solves neat, fascinating mysteries which never outrage belief. We may flatter ourselves that we have grown too sophisticated, but we are not yet too sophisticated for the late Ernest Bramah.
