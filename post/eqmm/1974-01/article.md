## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

This month, it must be acknowledged with regret, your mentor has floundered through worse than the customary shortage of good books.

“If not four worthy new items,” I said to myself at first, “surely there’ll be three?” Oh, no. “Two, then?” rang the silent cry. “It’s never failed; we’re bound to get at least two.” But we weren’t. “One, anyway! One measly book, one solitary title, to raise above the flood?” And then there were none.

There seems only one way out of this *impasse*. Some time ago I devoted an entire column to the late Anthony Berkeley Cox, an English friend who wrote both as Anthony Berkeley and as Francis Iles. The effort was well received; several correspondents asked for more reassessments of the same kind. And so, under the present circumstances, let’s glance back at an American mystery writer who bulked large on the horizon forty years ago: Willard Huntington Wright (1888-1939), fictionally known as S.S. Van Dine.

By profession an art and literary critic, plagued with constant ill-health, in the mid-1920s our protagonist planned a series of six detective novels. Each should have its formula title, the words “Murder Case” preceded by some descriptive term of six letters. Each as its amateur detective should feature Manhattan’s anglicized aristocrat and self-conscious intellectual—debonair, erudite Philo Vance.

Vance might prove a financial winner. Investigating psychologically, scoring all material clues, he would assist District Attorney Markham and doughty Sergeant Heath of Homicide.

Well, Van Dine had guessed right. Published by Scribners in 1926, *The Benson Murder Case* made an immediate hit. Its successor, *The “Canary” Murder Case*, boomed to still larger sales. Spouting learned lectures, Vance progressed through *The Greene Murder Case*, *The Bishop Murder Case*, and *The Scarab Murder Case*, five novels of the proposed half dozen. With book rights, with serial rights, with film rights for a relentless mill, Vance’s creator grew rich and famous at the time of the Great Depression. He might have stopped after *The Kennel Murder Case*, his sixth. By this time it was impossible to stop; the game had become too profitable. From whatever cause, perhaps poor health combined with the ghoulish influence of films, his hand faltered thereafter. He had chronicled a dozen cases when he died in '39. But, despite effective touches, real power had gone. His last two efforts seemed little more than querulous self-parody, an embarrassment to those of us who had admired.

Certain critics had always belittled the author because they disliked this particular sleuth. They whooped with glee at Vance’s mannerisms or his pseudo-Oxonian speech, although he carried it off as well as a genuine British product beloved by critics, H. D. Bailey’s Reggie Fortune. They ridiculed the aesthete both when he talked aesthetics and when he forsook talk for action. “Philo Vance,” they sang with the versifier, “needs a kick in the pance.” And too often, in that monotonous refrain, they ignored the strength of the story.

It is time for some more honest appraisal.

Now Van Dine knew his job. Ingenuity he had, adaptability—several ideas he found in Gross’s textbook, *Criminal Investigation*—as well as a vivid sense of character and immense narrative skill. He did nothing at random. Vance’s cultural lectures, by enemies condemned as extraneous, were in fact carefully crafted into the book’s texture and plan.

Judge him at his best. Try *The Greene Murder Case*, an epic of family slaughter in a decayed old mansion above the East River; try *The Bishop Murder Case*, which celebrates further mass homicide among mathematicians in the West Seventies. Both are brilliant; and the latter, with such sinister nursery overtones, must rank among the best detective novels ever written.

My own generation has its sentimental memories. As Sherlock Holmes belonged to boyhood, Philo Vance (far from effete, this fellow!) set a pattern for young men of the world. That Philo so often defeated the New York police troubled us no more than that Sherlock made a monkey of Scotland Yard. The light of youth illuminates both. Final analysis, it may be, will find Vance, Markham, and Heath not unworthy to drink a toast with Holmes and Watson as they all sit together in their Valhalla beyond the stars.
