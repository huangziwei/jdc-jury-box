## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Today the London Detection Club still flourishes as of yore. Having spoken much of that particular group, perhaps I may mention another projected club, just as excellent of intent, which never quite got off the ground—or came down from up in the air.

Also in London, one night towards the close of the nineteen thirties, some dozen or more of us assembled for dinner at the hospitable home of Fryn Tennyson Jesse and her husband, H. M. Harwood. Our purpose was to inaugurate a club for the study and discussion of picturesque murders from real life.

In one corner the most distinguished-looking man present, A. E. W. Mason of ambassadorial profile and silver hair, sat communing with a cigar. I remember Mrs. Belloc Lowndes, who was to read a paper on Lizzie Borden; garrulous Sir Hugh Walpole; still more garrulous Harry Hodge, Edinburgh publisher of the *Notable British Trials*; and benevolent Charles S. Evans, high khan at William Heinemann Ltd.

What should be the name of our group? Uproar broke out at once. Everybody called for something different, almost everybody was vociferous, and none of the vociferous would shut up. Worn down at length by Harry Hodge, who had edited a collection of gory prefaces called *The Black Maria*, we agreed to be the Black Maria Club. Then arose the question of whom we should admit.

“For instance,” somebody asked, “ought we to admit judges?”

Though I anticipated small difficulty here, thinking we might count ourselves lucky if we got any judges at all, objection had been entered by a stubborn soul who seemed to have based his views on the conduct of Judge Jeffreys. Majority vote finally decided that these miscreants might be admitted if they behaved.

“Need we confine ourselves to murder?” observed a gentle-faced lady. “Surely there are other crimes of equal fascination?”

Challenged to state one, she made several suggestions which the brethren greeted in a mood ranging from scorn to ribaldry. We argued this, we argued that, we went on arguing until a glance at the clock showed we had left time for nothing but to adjourn.

Out we went into the night. Subsequent suggestions of resuming activity produced little beyond more debate. Hitler's War intervened to wrench every life awry. The Black Maria Club does not exist; it never existed; the first meeting was also the last.

Such reflections have been inspired by the first of four items I would praise this month, the brilliant new biography of a man once powerful if shadowy—seeming in the American underworld during its most controversial age. Though I can offer you no first-class new fiction, let the biography be supported by three paperback novel reprints, all British: one comparatively recent fair-play detective story, one detective classic of forty years ago, and one classic of espionage from World War I.

In **Johnny Torrio, First of the Gang Lords**, by Jack McPhaul (Arlington House, $8.95), several violent generations have been reconstructed with the vividness of the veteran reporter and the care of the academic historian, for a story as *rip-roaring* as it is accurate.

Born near Naples in the early eighteen eighties, taken to New York as an infant, John Torrio was learning many ways of profit before in youth he left Manhattan for Chicago. He had learned the importance of controlling booze and owning brothels, though he neither drank, smoked, gambled, nor philandered. And Torrio himself never pulled a trigger; he just gave orders.

With the advent of prohibition he had gained great power, and went on to power still greater. Young Alphonse Capone, out from Brooklyn, was put to work as bouncer in one of Torrio's brothels, whence Capone rose to be chief lieutenant. Big Scarface Al, who exulted in publicity, took the spotlight for himself and left his boss in shadow according to the boss's wish. Once disaster brushed near, when O'Bannon's hoods tried to blast Torrio before Torrio's hoods did blast O'Bannon. J.T. survived, with only a scarred neck.

Survived? He tried retirement in Italy, and slipped back here only when Il Duce threatened to jail Yankee gangsters of Italian birth. And his luck held. After a heart attack at the barber's (1957), he died in peace, prosperity, and a hospital bed, at the mature age of 75. There may be a moral here, but it's not easy to find.

In **Let's Choose Executors**, by Sara Woods (Popular Library, 75¢), another tricky murder mystery inspires sensational courtroom drama for the talents of that very engaging barrister, Antony Maitland.

At ancient and provincial Chedcombe, on New Year's Eve, ancient and matriarchal Mrs. Randall has been poisoned after cutting immediate kin from her will and leaving a fortune to her hapless young goddaughter, Fran Gifford. Pinned in a corner by motive, opportunity, every semblance of guilt, Fran seems lost. The barrister originally defending her sees no line of protection; neither do the instructing solicitors.

But Antony Maitland, who can carry sympathy to the point of quixotry in juggling hot potatoes for a distressed client, accepts her innocence though he knows she's been telling lies. Here it's barrister-detective all the way, from clue to clue and a courtroom disclosure wholly credible. Outstanding; don't miss.

A twentieth-century Jack the Ripper plies his knife with full grisliness throughout Philip MacDonald's **Murder Gone Mad** (Avon, 95¢), first published just forty years ago.

Calling himself the Butcher, terrorizing ultra-respectable Holmdale, this maniac can't be nabbed by the deductions of the brilliant amateur, but only by organized police work. Therefore we bypass nerves-on-edge Colonel Gethryn; our protagonist is another old friend, Scotland Yard Superintendent Arnold Pike.

There are the suspects, named and lined up; which can it be? After several slips Pike's last ingenious trap snares the Butcher under circumstances as horrifying as they are dramatically effective. **Murder Gone Mad** has become murder gone sane.

Speaking of classics, it is time to reassess **The 39 Steps**, by John Buchan (Popular Library, 60¢), that early novel of espionage in the apparently settled world of Britain just before it burst into flames with the holocaust of 1914.

Much blood has flowed, many customs have altered, since those far-off days when the heroine of a spy chronicle did not strip off her clothes and jump into bed with the hero ten minutes after she met him. In fact, there is no heroine in the story. Told by Scottish-born Richard Hannay, who has returned to London from South Africa and become an innocent fugitive on the run, its wild doings sweep us across the Scottish moors, into comedy as well as danger, while Hannay tracks down the agents of the Black Stone.

John Buchan afterwards wrote more adventures of Richard Hannay and his friends, creating notable villains too: vicious Colonel von Stumm in *Greenmantle* or godlike Dominic Medina in *The Three Hostages*. But none has greater effect than the man who could hood his eyes like a hawk, and does so here until, in a literal sense, the whistle is blown at last.
