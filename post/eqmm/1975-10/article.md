## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Long ago, as faithful readers are aware, your mentor learned to be philosophic about books for review. Inane cheerfulness is as bad as constant griping; let’s avoid both. If I mayn’t have items divided between detection and espionage, say two from each field for balance, let them touch as many aspects of sensationalism as possible.

And that’s what has happened this time. Beginning with sound detection against a picturesque background, we go on to grand-scale crooks’ progress from long ago, continue through sophisticated cloak-and-dagger, and end with situation mystery in which the cry of “What’s going on here?” sings like a refrain. In short, there’s a little of everything.

Throughout **Bored to Death**, by Michael Delving (Scribners, $6.95), the only bore we encounter is the tidal rush of water so named from the River Severn, our setting for dirty work in a corner of rural England near which I once made my home.

Exploring the Gloucestershire countryside, Dave Cannon, American book-and-antique dealer with English wife, again meets trouble. Near the river he encounters a small silver-gilt casket, finely crafted mediaeval relic; in the river he encounters the murdered body of the casket’s unsavory owner.

The theft of the silver-gilt casket—did it ever really belong to Beaver?—only complicates a problem already confused enough. Superintendent Whimbrel, suspicious of everybody, has reason for suspicion until Dave Cannon stumbles on truth. The local types are well observed, the atmosphere authentic, the crime ingenious and with clues in full view. A-plus for Michael Delving.

The title of Michael Crichton’s **The Great Train Robbery** (Knopf, $7.95) refers to a heist imagined as challenging Scotland Yard under Queen Victoria, 1855, when railways provided the newest, fastest travel.

What a scheme! Much gold for British troops in the Crimea, packed into two safes alleged burglar-proof on a train London-to-Paris, is the target of suave, engaging, ruthless Edward Pierce, criminal genius, with allies both male-sinister and female-fetching. We watch not only through crooks’ eyes but through the eyes of the original Metropolitan Police, who *almost* penetrate Pierce’s plan before his ingenious trap for the train.

What happens then and afterward you must read for yourself; the author, no moralist, tells a rip-roaring story. Not least among this novel’s charms is the care in research which gives us persuasive Victorian spirit as well as speech. I enjoyed the book immensely, and I think you will enjoy it too.

In **The Safe House**, by Jon Cleary (Morrow, $7.75), much cloak-and-dagger play springs from an undercover game just after Hitler’s War, with two very different teams lined up against fate. As one team we have Europe’s displaced persons, many of them Jews trying to reach Palestine despite Britain, which will not yet encourage the new state of Israel. As another team we have Nazi war criminals, fleeing for sanctuary in South America.

Led by Luise Graz, beautiful Viennese singer with Catholic father and Jewish mother, our own small band of the displaced sets out for Italy *en route* to Israel, while American Major Dunleavy faithfully trails Luise. Also bound for Italy, among other Nazis, is the doctor who sent Luise’s mother to the gas chamber. Nazis and refugees must collide at their mutual “safe” house, the villa of an Italian countess who will accommodate both sides or any side for a price.

Collide they do, with results not altogether predictable in a house anything but safe, before the bullet-torn climax and satisfactorily happy ending of this fine thriller for your list.

With Michael Innes’ **The Mysterious Commission** (Dodd, Mead, $5.95), the creator of Sir John Appleby momentarily deserts his retired detective for an adventure in the world of art.

We face no usual problem, despite all the mystery. Charles Honeybath, fashionable portrait painter, is offered a large fee if he will visit somebody’s country house and do a portrait without learning where he is or whose picture he has been commissioned to paint.

It’s a criminal strategem; but, of course, what seems one sort of gambit is in fact something else. Honeybath himself develops talent as a sleuth, especially after meeting the girl from Imlac House, through exploits which show the author’s customary skill, humor, and charm. You can always trust Michael Innes.
