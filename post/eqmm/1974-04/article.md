## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

The cards must be played as they are dealt, leaving your mentor in his far from unusual dilemma of not knowing whether to laugh or swear. Our whole theme this month is biography, from the fancied to the real.

“Fancied” biography? Well, yes. All four books are for lovers of blood and thunder. Since preliminary remarks may only confuse, let’s get down to the dirty work at once. *Arma fabulamque cano*; you’ll soon understand.

If we begin with **James Bond, the Authorized Biography of 007**, by John Pearson (Morrow, $7.95), that’s fair warning. And its publishers have been equally fair. Despite the contention that Ian Fleming’s celebrated James Bond is very much a real secret agent on the international scene, this work has been subtitled “a fictional biography”; accept as much or as little as your own credulity may direct.

It’s a fascinating chronicle. As presented by Mr. Pearson, our protagonist whirls through adventures fully as sensational as any ever dreamed by the late Mr. Fleming. All-conquering James—always James; not even his mistresses ever endanger dignity by calling him Jim—remains aloof and imperturbable whether he is cutting cards, cutting capers, or cutting throats.

The women remain cooperative, the plotters ever devious when as Bond’s substitute we foil villainy through city after city, amid luxury to cushion menace and please the Walter Mitty in us all. Except for traitors, crooked gamblers, or the misguided K.G.B., it’s top of the tree, top of the morning, top of the world.

Some biographers have seemed uncertain regarding the whereabouts of Edgar Allan Poe from January to May 1844. Plausibly disarming, **A Singular Conspiracy**, by Barry Perowne (Bobbs-Merrill, $7.95), takes the hiatus as its premise for this colorful fantasy of an unrecorded chapter in Poe’s life.

Let’s accept the premise: he goes abroad. Assuming the name and identity of a wealthy young Southerner whom accident has left dead or dying on the New York waterfront, he heads for Paris to seek a commission in the Polish army during Poland's war with Russia. He finds no military glory, but he finds friends everywhere. Assisted by various literary men—including drug-ridden Charles Baudelaire, poet of evil—he precipitates a resounding domestic broil, a duel with stern old General Aupick, and a bloodless hoax whose outcome would have delighted him.

All ends well, even when the real Southern planter turns up to claim that fetching English girl. Turning towards home, wife, and mother-in-law, our own master of fantasy leaves behind, as one more fact to end noble romanticizing, harassed Charles Baudelaire translating Poe's works into French. If the adventure itself never happened, Mr. Perowne's ingenuity has supplied all needs.

And now for authentic biography in paperback print.

**The Worlds of Chippy Patterson**, by Arthur H. Lewis (Pocket Books, $1.25), through journalistic eyes studies a much-admired social phenomenon—the brilliant, erratic lawyer who sways juries to his will.

For Christopher Stuart Patterson, Jr. (1875-1933) there were three worlds: that of the wealthy, aristocratic family into which he had been born; that of the criminal courts at Philadelphia's City Hall; that of his own mind and heart, much harder to define.

“Chippy” (sometimes “Chiffy”) Patterson soon defied convention. Charm and passion he brought to the defense of any who consulted him, particularly the anti-respectable; nor had he any concern for his fee. At his best in a murder trial, he scored a dazzling record of acquittals and mild sentences, seldom losing a client to the chair.

Careless, unbuttoned Chippy Patterson both worked hard and played hard until a heart attack snatched him from the arms of his latest girl-friend before any alloted span of years. There is no need to point a moral; this story rears its own monument.

Probing many ill reputations in Renaissance Italy, Maria Bellonci's **The Life and Times of Lucrezia Borgia** (Pyramid, $1.50) features the fair-haired, much-married beauty, daughter of Pope Alexander VI and sister of the murderous Cesare Borgia; who survived both as Duchess of Ferrara. She her self must be found guiltless; if she knew of crime and treachery, she had no choice but to remain silent. Here move human beings in a glittering pageant which is also sound history; with so much steel or poison, let's avoid a vicious heroine when we can.
