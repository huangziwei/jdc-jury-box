## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

If prejudice may be allowed, very heartily I now dislike and distrust that word “suspense.” No doubt it had a certain cogency when Miss Kahn first applied it to the admirable Harper list. But today almost every attempt at mystery has come, rightly or wrongly, to be so described. If it’s a good mystery, that’s very high praise; let the publisher say so, disdaining weaker terms. Though I myself prefer “detective” novel, the highest praise, this word may be used only when there has been complete fair play with clues, a feature less rare than some critics appear to think.

Again we are to consider four titles, three novels and a book of unsolved enigmas from real life. Of the three fictions, one first novel and two second tries, each is a first-class mystery which plays strictly fair. At one stride, then, we have a whole trio of new candidates for homicidal honors. With great pleasure the old pastrycook welcomes them to the bakery.

Bill Pronzini’s **The Snatch** (Random House, $5.95) is told in the first person by a private investigator whose name we never learn, but whom we can welcome with the heartiest handshake. Though he professes admiration for the ancient pulp fictions of Hammett and Chandler, this unnamed sleuth bears no resemblance to dull, swollen-headed snarlers like Spade or Marlowe, whose famed “toughness” was mainly bad manners and who cultivated an unpleasant personality as their stock in trade. Mistakenly called Old Bear by his nagging girl friend, our protagonist forms an immensely likeable addition to the roster; it seems we shall meet him again.

When the nine-year-old son of Financial Speculator Louis Martinetti is kidnapped, with a ransom demand for $300,000, the boy’s frantic father has been warned that no member of the family must carry this money to any drop. In semi-isolation on the Peninsula with his wife and his few friends—Dean Proxmire, secretary, and Allan Channing, that fish-blooded tycoon—Martinetti summons Mr. No Name from San Francisco to make the drop.
