## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Last month, due to certain misunderstandings, I failed to sum up the year 1974. Hence the summing up of '74 must be done in this issue for January, '75.

Once more it was a so-so year. Under ideal circumstances I should prefer books almost equally divided between detection and mystery-thriller or mystery-adventure-espionage, with detection somewhat heavier in the balance. But that's a dream.

Although July gave us three admirable detective novels supported by one mystery-thriller, two other months, January and May, provided no worthy fiction of any kind. In January I avoided *the impasse* by discussing the best work of the late Willard Huntington Wright, alias S. S. Van Dine. In May a biography of America's ablest short-story writer among mystery mongers enabled me to commend his best work in some detail.

Biography, either factual or imaginative as fiction, rescued your mentor on still another occasion, supplying our entire list of titles for April. These biographies, real or fanciful, will be considered in due course. Meanwhile, allowing my own preference for fair-play detection of an ingenious plot, with that let retrospect begin.

*The Thirteenth Trick*, by Russell Braddon, keeps its reader on horns of doubt between only two suspects in mass murder. Swedish harness racing animates the many problems of Olle Hogstrand's *The Gambler*. We must contrast it with other racing complexities in *Slayride*, by Dick Francis, to watch an English investigator solve the murder mystery at a Norwegian track.

Speaking of contrasts, detection also flings baffling enigmas at two Bow Street Runners: one (Jeremy Sturrock) in 1800 with R. G. Jeffreys's *A Wicked Way To Die*; the other (Edmund Blackstone) in 1824 with *Beau Blackstone*, by Richard Falkirk.

Two modern masters have supplied new books, George Harmon Coxe at his best in *The Inside Man*, and Michael Innes similarly strong with *Appleby's Other Story*. As for similar past performances, I commended the Readers' Digest two-volume anthology, *Great Stories of Mystery and Suspense*, including a little gem by Ellery Queen, as well as *Murder on Board*, triple travel-homicide from Dame Agatha Christie. Before leaving detection, let's not forget Elizabeth Peters's *The Murders of Richard III*, in which this talented tyro counterfeits fifteenth century conditions and characters for her sound modern dilemma or dead-end.

Recommended mystery-thrillers? Well, yes.

Throughout *The Highbinders*, by Oliver Bleeck, the fabled sword of France's Crusader-Saint, King Louis IX, leads Philip St. Ives a dance of death through half-world London until the discovery of a hiding-place so clever as almost to defy detection. In *Swap*, by Walter Wagner, Captain David Garrison rescues a child hostage from the Russians after Yankee-Soviet clashes without espionage. In *Zaleski's Percentage*, by Donald MacKenzie, a Polish gentleman preserves his honor by restealing the jewelled relic he first stole in war-time long ago.

In *Pandora's Box*, by Thomas Chastain, robbery at the Metropolitan Museum soars to its startling climax; great art explodes a bomb of disaster with John Murphy's *The El Greco Puzzle*; Hal Gulliver's *Kill with Style* features uproar round a projected hotel for the island of Martinique; whereas my own favorite, *The Savage Day*, by Jack Higgins, gives us murderous double-cross in northern Ireland.

Though there were other mystery-thrillers, and good ones, let's open the door to espionage.

Space permits only brief praise for Lionel Black's *The Life and Death of Peter Wade*, Owen Sela's *The Kiriov Tapes*, Peter Driscoll's *In Connection with Kilshaw*, with special praise for Sam Durell in *Assignment—Amazon Queen*, by Edward S. Aarons, and for Matt Helm in Donald Hamilton's *The Intimidators*.

Fictional biography, as well as imaginary events to set off a real character? One has inspired *James Bond, the Authorized Biography of 007*, and the other some picturesque exploits for Edgar Allan Poe in Barry Perowne's *A Singular Conspiracy*.

Authentic biography? Among these let's concentrate on Charles A. Norton's *Melville Davisson Post: Man of Many Mysteries*, our most brilliant writer of the detective short story.

Perhaps I should add that the book I found most welcome was *Best "Thinking Machine" Detective Stories*, by Jacques Futrelle, also a volume of shorts from the French-named American who lost his life in the Titanic disaster of 1912.
