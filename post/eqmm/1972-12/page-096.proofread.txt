## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Glancing back over what I have written this year, I see that on no less than two occasions there has been time out for tribute. In July we honored my old friend Anthony Berkeley Cox, a grand master largely unrecognized. In September it was Anglo-Irish Sax Rohmer, remembering how tall stood Fu-Manchu amid the bogeymen of yesteryear. Apart from eulogy, and some few odd matters to be rounded out later, how shall we sum up 1972?

It went much as usual: neither feast nor famine, but with worthy items outweighing the inept. Both sides of the Atlantic provided a duo of homicidal ladies triumphant in straight detection.

Our own Emma Lathen, with *The Longer the Thread*, and the lady who writes as “Paul Kruger,” with *The Bronze Claws*, matched Ngaio Marsh’s *Tied Up in Tinsel* and *Season of Snows and Sins* by Patricia Moyes. To the same category belongs Sara Woods’s *And Shame the Devil*, in which Antony Maitland, barrister-detective, faces problems racial as well as criminal against a background of dourest Yorkshire.

Still! We needn’t have any author “match” another; since all these write well and play fair, it’s unnecessary. Good sleuthing, action-spiced, also animates Bill Pronzini’s *The Snatch*, Peter Lovesey’s *The Detective Wore Silk Drawers*, Hugh Pentecost’s *Birthday, Deathday*. But any prize for ingenious mystification must go to *The Japanese Mistress*, by Richard Neely, which, though seeming to hide nothing up its kimono sleeve, can still wallop our wits with a stunning surprise finish.

In the realm of the mystery-adventure-thriller, you are safe either with *The Curtained Sleep*, Archie Roy’s Scottish conflagration, or with *The Procane Chronicle*, Oliver Bleeck’s New York-Washington murder-go-round. Espionage too has been well served by Victor Canning (*Firecrest*), James Mitchell (*A Red File for Callan*), Berkely Mather (*The Terminators*), and Robert MacLeod (*Path of Ghosts*).
