## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Every critic must be on guard against moods. In private life we all allow for moods, momentarily disrupting judgment. When I began considering how for this December issue I might sum up 1973, my first impression was that it had been a very bad year. Then, as always, I glanced through back issues to review what I had written. And the impression had been a momentary mood.

Perhaps it arose from the fact that on several occasions, due to a surfeit of poor novels read and discarded, it was necessary to back up current items with classics from the past. But I need hardly remind you (or myself) that we assess quality only by the best. And in all three categories—detection, espionage, mystery-adventure—the best has been very good indeed.

Fair-play detection, *imprimis*, has provided both kinds of flesh and blood. In *Murder Without Icing*, by Emma Lathen, America's First Lady Baffler reveals the plight of the Sloan Guaranty Trust when that stately institution backs an ice-hockey team and provokes two murders for the detective wits of John Putnam Thatcher. In *Elephants Can Remember*, by Agatha Christie, once more an old crime throws its chilling shadow until Hercule Poirot dispels all menace. And with *Murder in Focus*, by Dorothy Dunnett, a compatriot of Dame Agatha explodes comedy-cum-murder among British skylarkers at an observatory in Rome.

This side of the Atlantic has seen three brilliant newcomers. Try Sylvia Angus's *Arson and Old Lace* for dirty work on the crime-bound Maine coast; in *A Dying Fall*, by Hildegarde Dolson, the more-than-dubious bridegroom plunges to death amid *avant-garde* statues; and, in Edith Taylor's *The Serpent Beneath It*, our dirty work paralyzes a quiet college campus. All three, homicidally speaking, will earn your trust.
