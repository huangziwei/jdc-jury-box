## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Last month I mentioned London’s Detection Club, of which only the late A. A. Milne was elected a full member on the strength of one novel alone. There have been various honorary members, including eminent writers who engage in crime or the sensational without employing straight detection, and police officials of the more exalted kind. The late Sir Hugh Walpole was an honorary member; so was a former Assistant Commissioner of the C.I.D. at Scotland Yard.

But, in general, the member must have written at least two detective novels conforming to certain rules. These rules are defined by an oath which the president, in scarlet robe and chain of office, administers to each new member on initiation. Part of the dialogue runs as follows:

“Do you solemnly swear never to conceal a vital clue from the reader?”

“I do.”

“Will you honor the King’s English?”

“I will.”

“Do you promise that your detectives shall well and truly detect, using only those wits with which you shall be pleased to endow them, and having no traffic with or placing no reliance upon accident, coincidence, feminine intuition, mumbo-jumbo, jiggery-pokery, or Act of God?”

“I do.”

“Will you preserve a seemly moderation in the use of conspiracies, gangs, death-rays, ghosts, trap doors, homicidal lunatics, and sinister Chinese, and utterly and forever forswear mysterious poisons unknown to science?”

Then, after the candidate has given other pledges, the president concludes with his terrible warning.

“This night, X.Y., you have sworn a great oath. If ever you do violate one article of that oath, may other writers anticipate your plots, may your publishers do you down in your contracts, may total strangers sue you for libel, may your pages swarm with misprints and your sales continually diminish.”

Well, they had the eccentric judgment to elect me in 1936. And since the oath is not secret, it can be quoted here without violating confidence or copyright.

Though I have attended no gathering of the brethren for more than a decade, membership in that club is for life. I am therefore entitled to wonder how its all-too-critical committee would regard today's omnipresence of cloak and dagger.

We have seen several devices not unlike death-rays, and there are more sinister Chinese than in the palmiest days of Sax Rohmer. The first-rate spy story, let's all agree, *need* not be constructed according to such purist principles as those above. On the other hand, what a joy when it can be and is! Of four titles heartily recommended this month, three are new and three are novels of espionage in the grand manner.

**The Smile on the Face of the Tiger** by Douglas Hurd and Andrew Osmond (Macmillan, $5.95) must be given high honors. Not only will it satisfy every rule of the Detection Club, but its carefully prepared surprise finale explodes like a mine. Though the main action has been set in the future, we are projected only to the easily imagined future of 1976.

Laurence Pershing, once a British junior during that past war in the Far East, is now a cabinet minister of the ruling Tory government. With an impossible wife but a charming mistress, he can look forward to still more distinction if he is not overtaken by a well-meant error out of his past. When the People's Republic of China demands that the British give up Hong Kong, sending tigerish Chiang Li-shih to London, the error does catch up and Pershing seems finished.

But he is not finished; Chiang Li-shih, the tiger, may have caught an infuriated lion with claws as deadly as his own. After deft move and counter-move, after convincing behind-the-scene glimpses of government both British and Chinese, the action whips at breakneck pace to its sensational end. No better story is likely to be published this year.

Whether or not you yourself have a touch of Scots blood, you will greatly sympathize with Secret Agent John Craig in **The Innocent Bystanders**, by James Munro (Knopf, $5.95). Employed by Department K, British Intelligence, Craig serves a devious M.I. boss who says one thing and means another, being almost as untrustworthy as the enemy.

Aaron Kaplan, Russian scientist imprisoned for independent thinking, has escaped from Siberia and disappeared. Somewhere in Turkey, Kaplan will defect to the West with his immensely valuable secret if either British or Americans can find him. But Russia wants him too; beware the K.G.B. In theory aided by two British agents he himself has trained, John Craig must rescue the lost scientist and bring him back alive.

Sent to New York for briefing, Craig has several brushes with the K.G.B. (or is it the C.I.A.?) before he gathers up attractive Miriam Loman and lights out for Istanbul. Mayhem, murder roll through the Middle East from Turkey to Cyprus. Everybody is double-crossing everybody else; the British agents themselves seem up to no good; and the person who appears most double-crossed proves the worst double-crosser of all. Let me commend you to another brilliant novel; you alone will never once be double-crossed.

Dorothy Gilman's **The Amazing Mrs. Pollifax** (Doubleday, $4.95) is American espionage with a bang. Don't be suspicious of any spy story written by a woman about a woman. Suspend disbelief in this middle-aged New Jersey widow as a C.I.A. agent, and you will accept Emily Pollifax with liking or even affection.

Once more we are off to Istanbul and the Turkish countryside. Abetted through spectacular exploits by a harum-scarum young Briton and an alleged Greek sailor, despite all violence Mrs. Pollifax has one hell of a good time. Depend on it, so will you.

No fine new straight detection being at hand, let's turn with relief to **The Rasp**, by Philip Macdonald (Avon, 95¢), that classic of the nineteen twenties with which a master craftsman gave us Colonel Anthony Ruthven Gethryn, formerly of the British Secret Service during World War I.

It's an ugly business. Cabinet Minister John Hoode has been messily slain, head battered with the heavy file called a rasp. Everybody looks suspicious because so few look suspicious, including a lovely dark-haired lady who swam the river at night.

Both Mr. Macdonald and Colonel Gethryn, happily, are still with us today. Gethryn has unravelled no tangle more ingenious than this, his maiden effort. Its colors gleam as brightly now as they shone more than forty years gone, when all life seemed simple and even we graybeards yet were spry.
