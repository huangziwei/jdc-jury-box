## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Whether or not symbolizing future luck, the new year continues to provide first-rate new books at the satisfactory rate of four a month. This time, beginning with one fair-play detective novel of classic pattern, we continue through two hypnotic sagas contrasting intelligent American and British police routine in their war against different but equally repulsive offenses, and end with the real-life biography of a high-flying, Brooklyn-born Italo-American who belongs to the royalty of crime.

Well, let’s get at ’em.

**Gently Through the Woods**, by Alan Hunter (Macmillan, $5.95), poses many questions of human behavior in another ingenious murder mystery for Detective Chief Superintendent Gently, Scotland Yard.

To do some personal filming by himself, Adrian Stoll, wealthy but erratic play-and-film director whose friends are almost all enemies, drives his Volkswagen camper into a patch of woodland near his country house. There he is poisoned by portable-cylinder gas, amid so many wishing his death that they might have stood in line outside the camper.

Maryon Britton, his mistress of many years, is being discarded for a young rival; Edwin Keynes, the cousin who took Maryon from him, will be disinherited if Stoll signs that new will. Youthful Lawrence Turner, talented painter, or old Oscar Walling more than shady financier: every suspect shows fangs of motive.

Despite complexities the author has devised a brilliant double solution to which every clue is honorably given. Superintendent Gently, tough but understanding, encounters one startling factor after another until he traps his quarry in a woodland-pursuit finish as sensational as it is dramatically effective. Mark this one A-plus.

Though the glib term “police procedural” has been applied to Rex Burns’s **The Alvarez Journal** (Harper & Row, $6.95), you must not imagine that investigation moves in any machinelike way, with lever or cogwheel predominant.

Chicano Detective Gabriel Wager, Denver Police, is almost sure that the Rare Things Import Shop, long suspected of smuggling in marijuana, has become a front for the sale of heroin. But can he prove it? The manager of the shop, facing him with polite enmity, is another clever Chicano, Rafael Alvarez, as slippery and crooked as Wager is human and honest.

Smaller fry clamor round them when Wager, aided by a colleague more concerned about his wife than about his career, sets bait for the big fish. It’s a fast, exciting story, firework-illuminated, in which the brains and guile of crookdom at last are overmatched by the brains and guile of the cops.

To nab a psychotic killer who preys on children, and win their case under laws which so often favor the guilty, is the challenge for Britain’s C.I.D. throughout **The Evidence You Will Hear**, by Hamilton Jobson (Scribners, $6.95).

In some thriving if unnamed town three girls aged six to nine have already been abducted, sexually attacked, and slain when small Angela Murray fails to return from school. Though Angela’s fate remains in doubt, all omens look sinister. Rage and panic blaze together as this sober community faces the reality of their own madman a-prowl.

Our title suggests only the courtroom, but much action unrolls before any jury confronts repellent young Peter Mellish, who still sneers at his captors and thinks himself above the law. This psycho plans escape if they convict him; Angela’s father swears vengeance in any case. And yet, despite so many grim threats, there’s happiness at the end of as vivid and gripping a novel as you will read this year.

Since author and publisher offer Brian David Boyer’s **Prince of Thieves** (Dial Press, $8.95) as mainly factual, apart from changed names, let’s accept that assessment.

Vivid from start to finish is this biography of free-spending Peter (“Tony”) Milano, described as the world’s greatest forger. Usually in disguise, as much actor as forgery artist, he “moved” stolen bonds or securities all over Europe. For Brooklyn Tony it was the sweet life of luxury until somebody betrayed him.

Even after he had served time he couldn’t change his life. Read for yourself how he was blackmailed into one last forgery, a fittingly ironic climax for any artist’s career.
