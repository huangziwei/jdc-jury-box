## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Though we have entered that sluggish season, pre-fall, when by tradition publishers hesitate to sponsor worthy sensationalism, your mentor can find no cause for complaint. Heartily I welcome any whirl of the merry-go-round which provides two lively detective novels, one superior mystery-adventure, and one Victorian saga exploring murky byways during the reign of the Good Queen. Enough for prelude; let’s go.

Wit and sophistication, animating colorful or eccentric personalities in a picturesque Connecticut town, mark **A Dying Fall**, by Hildegarde Dolson (Lippincott, $5.95), as one of the best mysteries this year.

During the lifetime of her domineering tycoon father, handsome Grace Dilworth has reached mature years without learning much about men or their ways. Inheriting millions on her father’s death, Grace returns home from a long cruise with Davin Lowry, the impressive, theatrically handsome stranger she has determined to marry.

About Davin Lowry people can’t make up their minds. Fortune-hunter or honest man, real or phony, loving Grace or with other plans? Then besotted Grace and her bridegroom-to-be announce their intention of destroying the town’s cherished rose garden by turning it into a display ground for avant-garde statuary. Soon afterwards Lowry pitches down the steps into the rose garden, there to be found dead and impaled on a horrendous exhibit.

Retired Inspector McDougal and Lucy Ramsdale, that likable if cat-curious widow, investigate throughout tension by day and terror at night until a very vicious killer is snatched out from behind the screen of roses in this ingenious, polished performance by another candidate for high honors in the Ladies’ Baffle Brigade.

From the first chapter of **Naked to the Grave**, by Harry Carmichael (Saturday Review Press, $5.95), when Julian Davey’s lovely, faithless wife is slain and mutilated with a pair of scissors in the bedroom of the Hampstead flat, suspicion points straight at Julian Davey himself. It’s another problem for the investigating team of Piper, insurance assessor, and Quinn, ultra-imaginative newspaperman, in a whirligig of easy morals and easy money.

Hired to unearth evidence of Davey’s innocence, Piper seems only to find evidence of Davey’s guilt. But doors open into other motives, other lives; and what *did* the old woman see? Though it may not be the author’s best story, Mr. Carmichael writes with such persuasiveness that he lures you into his grip and won’t let go; you can’t stop reading until you learn the truth.

From the dark shadows of **Postscript to a Dead Letter**, by Donald MacKenzie (Houghton Mifflin, $5.95), France’s *Union Corse*, the criminal brotherhood like one on our own shores, stretches tentacles across Paris to crush and destroy.

Radnor Brown, hapless young American, has stumbled on evidence of the brotherhood’s traffic in heroin; it lands him only in jail on a false charge. Breaking out to join Phoebe, his attractive sister, young Brown finds a swashbuckling champion in Ross Macintyre, Canadian reporter for the London *Post*.

Aided by George Gunn, an old pal; by Ilinka Ostravo, that surprising lady from Russia; and by Misty Farrell, the reporter’s own Girl Friday, Ross Macintyre sets out to prove his belief through adventures loaded with high explosive at every step.

Our Canadian Scot won’t be beaten. Past booby-traps set by cops, robbers, robber cops, and robbers in government, Macintyre batters his way to the confrontation at, of all places, a convent with a very strange Reverend Mother. And Donald Mackenzie, himself a Canadian Scot, has added another brilliant mystery-adventure to his already impressive list.

In a stuffily curtained mansion of London, 1890, Jean Stubbs’s monumental **Dear Laura** (Stein and Day, $6.95) erupts with bitter domestic clashes leading to death by poison. Though there seem to be familiar elements—Laura, the beautiful young wife; Theodore, the cold and too-reserved husband; Titus, the dashing younger brother—yet no event here follows any familiar pattern; we may expect only the unexpected.

When investigation proves Theodore’s death to have been suicide and not murder, can any author sustain a fever of interest for almost three hundred pages? And yet Miss Stubbs does just that. Surprise piles on surprise until, with one last twist of the knife, we are left with a cumulation which must be called satisfactory because it is so obviously inevitable.
