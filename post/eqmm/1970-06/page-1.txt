THE JURY BOX

by JOHN DICKSON CARR

Last month I mentioned London’s Detection Club, of which only the late A. A. Milne was elected a full member on the strength of one novel alone. There have been various honorary members, including eminent writers who engage in crime or the sensational without employing straight detection, and police officials of the more exalted kind. The late Sir Hugh Walpole was an honorary member; so was a former Assistant Commissioner of the C.I.D. at Scotland Yard.

But, in general, the member must have written at least two detective novels conforming to certain rules. These rules are defined by an oath which the president, in scarlet robe and chain of office, ad-ministers to each new member on initiation. Part of the dialogue runs as follows:

“Do you solemnly swear never to conceal a vital clue from the reader?”

“I do.”

“Will you honor the King’s English?”

“I will.”

“Do you promise that your detectives shall well and truly detect, using only those wits with which you shall be pleased to endow them, and having no traffic with or placing no reliance upon accident, coincidence, feminine intuition, mumbo-jumbo, jiggery-pokery, or Act of God?”

“I do.”

“Will you preserve a seemly moderation in the use of conspiracies. gangs, death-rays, ghosts, trap doors, homicidal lunatics, and sinister Chinese, and utterly and forever forswear mysterious poisons unknown to science?”

Then, after the candidate has given other pledges, the president concludes with his terrible warning.
