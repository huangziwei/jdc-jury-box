## Best Mysteries of the Month

recommended by John Dickson Carr

Yes, it was bound to happen. Your mentor, that cynical old so-and-so, should have known better than to voice glowing hopes for 1970; this month almost inevitably finds us with only one first-class new mystery in sight.

So cashiered, busted a prophet should do some penance for his rashness. What penance, though? To wear sackcloth and ashes would be classically appropriate but impractical. If I called at *EQMM*'s office in any such ill-chosen costume, they would probably throw me out as a freak.

And yet all is not lost. Despite that solitary hardcover, there are rescuing cavalry trumpets from three paperbacks; one new spy thriller and two fact-crime items in reprint. Let these make the old man's penance less severe.

In **Puppet on a Chain**, by Alistair MacLean (Doubleday, $5.95), even the puppets have murderous intent. Each of Mr. MacLean's books, whether of wartime or of peacetime riot, has contained some element of mystery. Here mystery is as important as violence, with ingenious narcotics-pushing whose agents can't be traced. Any reader disposed to regard narcotics-pushing as a theme too bewhiskered should see how it can be done when it's done as it ought to be.

Major Paul Sherman, one of the British heads of Interpol, has been urgently summoned to Amsterdam by his most trusted contact in Holland. Landing at Schiphol with two female assistants, beautiful dark-haired Maggie and beautiful blonde Belinda, Sherman expects anything except to see his contact shot down in the airport before they can meet.

Thenceforward, against grotesque backgrounds from night club to church to sinister warehouse (even that luxury hotel is sinister), the pace erupts with a gun-fight at the docks, a night chase over the Zuider Zee, and a final confrontation in the sky. Sherman himself strews the path with corpses before he sees the truth at a haymakers' dance and expounds it just in time. Experienced readers should spot the central villain, partly from one broad clue which Sherman does not even mention in summing up. To call this a brilliant book is only to stress the fact that its author has played fair.

With **The Dragon's Eye**, by Scott C. S. Stone (Fawcett, 60¢) it's spies and action, action and still more spies. Michael Hawkins—no secret agent; formerly a working newsman in the Far East—has retired to Hawaii on his modest savings, seeking peace and quiet. But Hawkins's friend Leslie Trent, British secret agent as well as newsman, uses a kind of mental judo to pry him loose.

From behind the Bamboo Curtain, then, they will rescue Malcolm Leigh, once a Chicom sympathizer deep in Maoist counsels, who now wants no more of it and must be smuggled out to tell what he knows. But Leigh won't trust anybody except Hawkins, and won't move anywhere unless they also rescue his Chinese mistress, Choy-lin.

Beginning with near-disaster in Hong Kong, the two adventurers encounter dirty work aplenty before they meet Leigh and Choy-lin. Then it's more ground to cover, more enemies to dodge, in a spectacular race that never slackens until the burst of gunplay at the finale. Civilized reading, worth your time and attention.

Passing from fiction to fact, in Arthur H. Lewis's **Lament for the Molly Maguires** (Pocket Books, 95¢) we have the most balanced, authoritative account of terror and murder waged by one branch of an Irish secret society in the anthracite coal fields of Pennsylvania during the eighteen seventies, ending only when a Pinkerton detective infiltrated their ranks and broke their power. On these facts Conan Doyle based his Sherlock Holmes novel, *The Valley of Fear*; interpretation of the facts can still spark controversy almost a century later.

“Hostilities between the Molly Maguires and the (mine) owners,” writes Mr. Lewis, “were not battle between capital and labor . . . In vain have Molly apologists tried to intertwine their protagonists and the labor movement: The facts do not bear this out.”

Nothing extenuating, nor setting down aught in malice, the author avoids mere sensationalism as such; the story itself is sensational enough to have no need of colors more lurid. His whole chronicle, sombre and powerful, will hold you until the end.

For those wanting to learn how secret agents behave in real life, as opposed to how they behave in cloud-cuckooland, **The Spies**, edited by Robert G. Deisdorfer (Fawcett, 75¢) provides thirteen cases (count 'em, thirteen) of the real thing. Here are cloak-and-dagger deeds by friend, by enemy, and by the uncommitted, in two world wars as well as various cold wars and one war beyond our scope: the six-day affair of Arab-Israeli. From debunked Mata Hari to swaggering Che Guevara it's strong stuff, highly recommended. And yet in my heart, *mea maxima culpa*, I still prefer cloud-cuckooland.
