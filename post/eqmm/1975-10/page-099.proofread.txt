Paris, is the target of suave, engaging, ruthless Edward Pierce, criminal genius, with allies both male-sinister and female-fetching. We watch not only through crooks’ eyes but through the eyes of the original Metropolitan Police, who *almost* penetrate Pierce’s plan before his ingenious trap for the train.

What happens then and afterward you must read for yourself; the author, no moralist, tells a rip-roaring story. Not least among this novel’s charms is the care in research which gives us persuasive Victorian spirit as well as speech. I enjoyed the book immensely, and I think you will enjoy it too.

In **The Safe House**, by Jon Cleary (Morrow, $7.75), much cloak-and-dagger play springs from an undercover game just after Hitler’s War, with two very different teams lined up against fate. As one team we have Europe’s displaced persons, many of them Jews trying to reach Palestine despite Britain, which will not yet encourage the new state of Israel. As another team we have Nazi war criminals, fleeing for sanctuary in South America.

Led by Luise Graz, beautiful Viennese singer with Catholic father and Jewish mother, our own small band of the displaced sets out for Italy *en route* to Israel, while American Major Dunleavy faithfully trails Luise. Also bound for Italy, among other Nazis, is the doctor who sent Luise’s mother to the gas chamber. Nazis and refugees must collide at their mutual “safe” house, the villa of an Italian countess who will accommodate both sides or any side for a price.

Collide they do, with results not altogether predictable in a house anything but safe, before the bullet-torn climax and satisfactorily happy ending of this fine thriller for your list.

With Michael Innes’ **The Mysterious Commission** (Dodd, Mead, $5.95), the creator of Sir John Appleby momentarily deserts his retired detective for an adventure in the world of art.

We face no usual problem, despite all the mystery. Charles Honeybath, fashionable portrait painter, is offered a large fee if he will visit somebody’s country house and do a portrait without learning where he is or whose picture he has been commissioned to paint.

It’s a criminal strategem; but, of course, what seems one sort of gambit is in fact something else. Honeybath himself develops talent as a sleuth, especially after meeting the girl from Imlac House, through exploits which show the author’s customary skill, humor, and charm. You can always trust Michael Innes.
