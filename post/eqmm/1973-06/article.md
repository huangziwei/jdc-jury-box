## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

The summer doldrums are upon us, drying up most sources. For your entertainment, therefore, I must both begin and end with a time-tested classic: the first a novel featuring our earliest and best scientific detective, the second a book of short stories snake-fanged with clever plots. Between these we shall consider one roaring modern mystery-adventure with espionage overtones, and one modern detective story against so unlikely a background as Finland. Under such circumstances, after all, the doldrums might be worse.

If you are unfamiliar with **The Red Thumb Mark**, by R. Austin Freeman (Norton, $4.50), try the hardcover reissue. First published in 1907, this little masterpiece marked the debut of Dr. John Evelyn Thorndyke, urbane medico-legal investigator, conjuring up practical wizardry at King's Bench Walk.

When young Reuben Hornby is accused of robbing his uncle's safe, with evidence of a bloody thumbprint incontestably his own, even the young man's solicitor advises him to plead guilty and beg for mercy; only Dr. Thorndyke can conceive a defense.

Some unseen presence has determined to eliminate Thorndyke; the attempts on his life are so devilishly ingenious as to be almost foolproof. But the sage can't be trapped. In open court he smashes the Crown's case, nailing the true culprit as he presides over the romance between worthy Dr. Jervis and charming Juliet Gibson. Any innocent who appeals to John Thorndyke is as safe as though he had appealed to Sherlock Holmes.

As a new item in the fast-paced, shoot-'em-up style of mystery-adventure, let there be praise for **The Bearer Plot**, by Owen Sela (Pantheon, $5.95). Nicholas Maasten, London anti-hero of several business ventures, has a certain iron honesty even at dirty work. When from a dubious friend he buys rare postage stamps which may or may not have been stolen, he hopes to reap rich profit by selling the stamps to a nobleman in Spain.

Instead Maasten wallops straight into somebody's deadly plot, replete with bearer bonds, gold bars, and a Nazi treasure buried in a lake. Racing across Europe, threat-pursued, he is driven not only by two lovely ladies but by the British cloak-and-dagger bureau which still holds him for blackmail. And behind every machine-pistol lurks that sinister cripple in the wheel-chair.

If Anti-hero Maasten seems hairier and more mod than most readers can approve, he is a good fellow who stands by his friends through any extremity, including a scuba-diving fight in icy water. At the finale he emerges—if not triumphant, at least with profit enough to carry him (let’s hope) through another adventure fully as good as this one.

Mistaken souls who regard the people of Finland as heavy or stodgy, considering Helsinki as anything but Fun City, will find in **The Trevick Affair**, by Mauri Sariola (Walker, $4.95), a wild detective impasse disrupting the lives of sympathetic human beings whose pulses beat only too fast.

To Osmo Kilpi, idealistic young lawyer, comes the quiet middle-aged couple whom persecution would drive from their modest market garden in the country. Though Arvid Teerisuo and his wife have offended nobody, some unknown enemy burns down their sauna and wrecks their living room with a shotgun. Then murder removes Teerisuo in another fire.

But perplexity deepens when the victim’s wife suddenly blossoms into a swinging widow with a young boy friend. Osmo Kilpi and his shapely secretary, Irja Hartti, are swept into a whirl of death which culminates in sensational disclosure.

In conclusion, good brethren, I give you Jacques Futrelle’s **The Thinking Machine**. Also belonging to *annus mirabilis* 1907, it contains seven exploits of Professor Augustus S.F.X. Van Dusen, aided by Reporter Hutchinson Hatch. In “The Problem of Cell 13,” a classic, Professor Van Dusen bets he can escape from the condemned cell of a prison; before winning his bet he all but drives the warden mad. Though the other tales can’t match this *chef-d’oeuvre*, both “The Scarlet Thread” and “The Flaming Phantom” employ dirty work as unexpected as it is horribly simple.

Jacques Futrelle, despite his Gallic name, was an American newspaperman who fashioned his plots against the staid setting of Boston, Mass., and perished with the *Titanic* in 1912. **The Thinking Machine** is not easy to find; but, believe me, it’s worth the search.
