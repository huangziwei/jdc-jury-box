## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

This month, the year’s halfway mark, we celebrate two first-rate detective novels, one roaring spy thriller and one study in Sherlockian biography which will hold particular fascination for any reader who admires the greatest sleuth of them all.

In **The Longer the Thread** by Emma Lathen (Simon & Schuster, $5.95), once more the business judgment of the Sloan Guaranty Trust must be vindicated by John Putnam Thatcher, senior vice-president and all-purpose detective.

They have backed Harry Zimmerman, Seventh Avenue garment manufacturer, whose American plant hums at high prosperity but whose accident-plagued new factory in Puerto Rico (sabotage, no doubt?) seems headed for a smash. Thatcher arrives to investigate; the suspected saboteur, Foreman Benito Dominguez, is nastily slain on premises to which few have access.

Then festival, holocaust, and more murder. Caught up into it are Norma Lippert, Harry Zimmerman’s sister; David, her moody husband; conscientious Cesar Aguilera; a raucous student agitator called Prudencio; Annie Galiano, engaging female labor leader trusted both by labor and management alike. Since Miss Lathen plays fair, study the evidence; make sure the author is really saying what she seems to be saying. Legitimate misdirection may obscure but can’t hide the essential clue to a murder mystery both ingenious and terrifying from the first whisper in Wall Street to that last grisly plunge from the battlements. Here’s Thatcher at his best.

By sheer accidental cussedness Paul Kruger’s **The Bronze Claws** (Simon & Schuster, $4.95) was issued by the same firm which published the Emma Lathen novel above. This can’t be helped; I must take what comes, and play ‘em as they’re dealt.

Phil Kramer, Colorado’s young lawyer-investigator, here tackles a case which seems to make no sense. In his search for the missing brother of beautiful if improbably named Holly Wood, our young lawyer races from business community to hippie haunt, from the home of Dr. David Castle to a burnt-out mountain retreat where Dr. Castle himself seems to have died by accident in the fire. The doctor’s widow, though she has collected half-million-dollar insurance, still lives as though pressed for cash and won’t marry the man she so clearly wants.

Allowing fraud, of what sort and by whom? “Paul Kruger,” as in four previous books, has striven not only for the twist, but for a double and even triple twist to bring down the curtain. Whether you anticipate these shocks or are flummoxed by them, you should enjoy every page along the way.

For cloak-and-dagger capers against an exotic background, you won’t do better than the espionage seesaw of Berkely Mather’s **The Terminators** (Scribners, $5.95).

Almost throwing away its third-person prologue, in which a Miami-bound jet is hijacked to Havana by unidentified pirates for a purpose unexplained, this bullet-spattered chronicle shifts to the first person and to India’s northern frontier at the very crux of trouble.

Idwal Rees, Britain’s secret agent-narrator, must seize and bring back a Russian defector, Palinovsky of the K.G.B., on whom every side in the cold war wants to lay hands. Rees must do this despite obstacles provided by his own immediate boss; the unspeakable Gaffer, and by his strong-minded *inamorata*, Claire Culverton.

The author, formerly a British officer stationed in India, has captured native character as well as native atmosphere. And it all works out, if in no anticipated way. Palinovsky is packed off to America; Claire bids Rees a final good-bye; and, since all our sympathies have been with him, it’s a very happy ending.

Whatever your feelings towards Sherlock Holmes and Dr. John H. Watson, so long associated at 221B Baker Street, you will welcome **The Histrionic Holmes**, by Marvin Kaye (Luther Norris, $4.00).

You may be one of those omniscient scholars who offhand can answer any canonical question: “Where was ‘126B?’” for instance. Or you may retain from boyhood only vague, pleasurable memories of deductions by gaslight and hansoms rattling through the fog.

But all human beings love disguise. This study of Holmes’s offstage roles—“You would have made an actor, and a rare one,” declared Inspector Athelney Jones—is more than compendium or analysis; it is a practical handbook of the whole subject. If I cannot accept the suggestion that Holmes impersonated still another character from a different saga, Mr. Kaye’s urbane, reasoned book has one more justification; it is good entertainment on its own.
