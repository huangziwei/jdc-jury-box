Apparently to help, the general sends Fighter Pilot Major Bill Bradley to find the missing man and hide him until this rumpus dies. Convinced of Sergeant Kirk's innocence, Bradley also becomes convinced that Willie Joe will be captured or gunned down before help can arrive. Assisted by Beth Ferguson, friend of the slain girl, Bradley plunges into explosive cross-country adventure climaxed by an air-sea rescue-trap which provides climax and explanation for a first-class problem in murder.

John D. MacDonald's **Bright Orange for the Shroud** (Lippincott, $5.95) again features likable Travis McGee, that somewhat tarnished knight errant whose affectation of cynicism never stops him from rescuing the helpless. Well-meaning Arthur Wilkinson has been robbed of his fortune by confidence crooks whose decoy lured Arthur into marriage.

It's a battle of giants when Travis McGee goes after 'em: crooks suave, crooks crude, and in particular Boone Waxwell, the most repulsive hobgoblin of recent fiction. Though by design the author offers little mystery, as usual he snares us with a roaring good story which grips at once and won't let go until the end.

John Miro, retired secret agent of Shaun Herron's **Through the Dark and Hairy Wood** (Random House, $5.95), now asks for little more than to live at peace with wife and son on his farm in republican Ireland.

But peace has been denied. Crossing the northern border to buy farm stock, at once he is swept up amid flying bullets and knives behind every hedgerow. With scant sympathy for either side, Miro fights to get home alive. How he solves the riddle of enigmatic Captain Strong adds impetus to a first-class adventure which is both full-blooded and full of blood in the best tradition.

Now for fact. **Cheap Thrills**, “An Informal History of the Pulp Magazines,” by Ron Goulart (Arlington House, $7.95) graphically recreates the once mighty pulp empire, whose gaudy colors blazed from every newsstand until various economic factors destroyed it after World War II.

Cheap only in being modestly priced, it provided excitement for the youthful or the stay-at-home, instilling a morality as rigid as any Sunday School's. Despite stories of every kind, love or sport or war, so many featured mystery that we may consider this category on its own. Naive plots or not, perhaps something may be lacking in a day when fiction has become only an interlude between commercials. *Ave atque vale*; likewise, *eheu fugaces*!
