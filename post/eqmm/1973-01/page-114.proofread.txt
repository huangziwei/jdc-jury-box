## The Jury Box
by John Dickson Carr

Once more, of necessity, January’s introductory assessment will contain a warning that most items here considered must belong to the old year rather than the new. Never mind; it’s a very fair complement of blood and thunder. I can provide one espionage exploit of such superior quality as to deserve pride of place; two detective novels contrasted in their approach; and one collection of classic tales, time *circa* Edwardian, here at last rescued from oblivion. With such strong fare to claim attention until we are properly launched for '73, not a soul among us need complain.

Swift, deadly move or counter-move throughout William P. McGivern’s **Caprifoil** (Dodd, Mead, $6.95) finds American Agent August Spencer and Britain’s likable “Bunny” Ackerley locked in combat against unseen forces, their lives always in hazard with every hand against them.

André Leroi, their former associate and France’s own ace agent, has disappeared with a warning of disaster which may soon explode. Against earth’s security Arab fanatics called the Saracen Vector have devised a plot so wild yet so practical as almost to stagger imagination.

Can Spencer thwart the design? Can anybody thwart it? For in action there’s no pause or break. Not only do we race with Spencer from Paris to Frankfurt and back again, while death ticks like a bomb at his ear. Adroit shifts of narrative carry us from the White House in Washington to the terrorists’ stronghold outside Algiers, where Arab Colonel Gamal awaits triumph with General Mercier, the French traitor who backs him.

But nemesis has been challenged. As the American aircraft carrier approaches Algiers, as the sky thunders to jets or darkens with descending parachutes, one last private battle sprays bullets through the Arabs’ fort to write *finis*. August Spencer survives, taking wing to join his latest mistress in Mexico. And, since Mr. McGivern is a hypnotic story-teller, we have believed every word along the way.
