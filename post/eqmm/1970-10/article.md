## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Another Detection Club anecdote, if you don't mind. At the club's annual dinner in May of 1938, as already stated, our principal speaker was the barrister, Norman Birkett, K.C., who soon showed that he enjoyed nothing more than to appear for the defenseless and even the indefensible. But that is neither the beginning nor the end of the story.

Being honorary secretary at this time, I had been instructed by the committee to approach Mr. Birkett with a view of getting him there. And I acquired much credit among the brethren when I reported success. Somebody (not myself) started the whisper that I had lured him into our toils by a Machiavellian plot with overtones of blackmail. Though it would delight me to confirm this, nothing of the sort happened. All I did was write to the gentleman, introduce myself, and invite him. He replied, as promptly as courteously, that he had heard of our club and would be happy to accept, his sole stipulation being that we entertain no member of the press.

The first problem arose on the night of the dinner. I had never met Norman Birkett or seen his photograph; neither had anybody else. My wife was posted in the foyer at Grosvenor House to greet the person who in her judgment must be our guest. Now my wife cherished the belief, as I myself did, that any eminent K.C. would be barrel-shaped like Sir Henry Curtis Bennett. After several times accosting the wrong man, she feared lest someone misconstrue her motives and call a cop. Mr. Birkett, who found us instead, proved to be long, lean, red-haired, and very amiable.

He had scarcely commenced his indiscreet speech before I knew with alarm that I should never have guaranteed the absence of the press. True, no reporters had been invited. But each club member could bring as many guests as he liked, supplying only their names for the seating arrangements. At the very next table might lurk some eager beaver from the *Daily Express*, who would plaster counsel's remarks all over page one.

I need not have worried; no disaster ensued. And yet alarm very nearly became panic. Following the dinner, still more or less in public, our guest related to some of us the outcome of the celebrated murder trial in which he had successfully defended a notorious wrong 'un whose identity shall not be disclosed here. Let's call him Herbert Rumpus, a name unlike his own. His counsel, using real names, quoted Rumpus's words when the jury brought in their verdict of acquittal.

“Though he was speaking in a hushed whisper,” counsel said, “his thunderous incredulity seemed to ring through the courtroom as he blurted out: ‘Not guilty, Mr. Birkett? *My God, not guilty?*’”

Like the end of a good detective story, at least it had the merit of being unexpected.

That quality of the unexpected has large place in the various items we shall consider this month. Though there are only three books, the first will be an omnibus containing a trio of good detective novels, the second is galloping mystery-adventure with a neat twist at its finale, the third a collection of real-life oddities ranging from vampirism to buried treasure. In short, here's variety as well as quality; fall to!

In **Archer At Large**, by Ross Macdonald (Knopf, $7.95), we find *The Galton Case*, *The Chill*, and *Black Money*, all featuring one of the few tough private investigators with whom everybody can sympathize. Unlike the sneering, snarling heroes from Hammett or Chandler, Lew Archer is a decent sort who does not make bad manners the guiding principle of his life.

Ross Macdonald being a writer whose work has improved as he himself has matured, each novel is a genuine detective story as well as a genuine thriller; and Mr. Macdonald has one gift, ingenuity, denied those with whom he is usually compared. If *Black Money* may be the most polished performance among these three, the shock ending of *The Chill*, while perfectly fair, has been spun with such craft as almost to defy detection. Whether Lew Archer pursues a lost heir, a lost bride, or a presumed Frenchman with doubtful antecedents, let his path lie amid the unco’ rich through the groves of academe, the ultimate move will be suitably violent and suitably satisfactory. Don’t miss a trick; the author never does.

About Leo Martin, protagonist-narrator in **The Amphorae Pirates**, by Lou Cameron (Random House, $4.95), the reader at first learns little except that Martin seems to be a free-lance writer and experienced scuba-diver. Various characters, mostly unlicensed, have been diving in Italian waters not only for amphorae, big earthenware jars which can be sold as antiques, but for gold and silver booty from long-sunken ships of Greece or Rome. In head-on collision Martin smacks against one such pirate, the big Hawaiian named Mike Konakai, with his vicious assistant and his Negro mistress.

Martin next encounters rich Dr. Menderes, who has a daughter as handsome as she is broad-minded, a genuine American passport, and a genuine salvage license from the Italian government. In his search for the most ancient of treasure-galleys the doctor already employs a diver, sinister Tancredi, but takes on our well-meaning narrator (is he so well-meaning?) for additional strength. Martin accepts this job with the connivance of big Mike Konakai, who will hijack the treasure once Dr. Menderes has found it. And watching everybody stands suspicious-minded Captain Colonna, Italian Navy, alert for dirty work against his country's interest.

The whole sea boils with underwater dirty work, including assorted murders, before tranquillity can be fished up from wreck. Here we have notable mystery-adventure well supported with clues to its surprise finish. Four stars and three cheers.

**Mischief in the Mountains**, edited by Walter R. Hard, Jr., and Janet C. Greene (Vermont Life Magazine, $5.95), has been aptly subtitled 'Strange Tales of Vermont and Vermonters.' For Green-Mountain Vermont, believe it or not, has not always bred the thrifty, laconic Yankee of popular legend. If it could produce Calvin Coolidge, it could also produce such flamboyant figures as 'Colonel' Jim Fisk, wildcat financier, and 'Colonel' George Harvey of newspaper and publishing fame. Finally, it could produce the individualists whose doings enliven this book.

They are far from being inhibited. The town of Chester had a gentleman burglar, Clarence Adams, who in true Deacon-Brodie style robbed his neighbors blind until the spring-gun trapped him at Waterman's Mill. Woodstock Green had, or thought they had, a vampire like Dracula. Whatever the truth of the matter, they dug him up to burn his heart in public. And old Mercie Dale so successfully cursed the Hayden family that there are no Haydens left. You would do well to browse through these tales. Not all concern crime; but, as Sherlock Holmes would have said, "there are some very pretty little problems among them." Good!
