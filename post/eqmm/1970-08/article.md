## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Quite unintentionally, be assured, I blundered and misled you. It is hard to understand in what strange absence of mind or loss of mind I called A.A. Milne the only writer ever elected to the London Detection Club on the strength of one novel alone. And I did it twice, in successive months, without realizing what had been said. If I looked up from the typewriter, I could have seen among books against the opposite wall a copy of E.C. Bentley’s *Trent’s Last Case*.

*Trent’s Last Case* had established itself as a detective classic before the outbreak of World War I. When Anthony Berkeley founded the Detection Club in 1932, E.C. Bentley became a charter member. Our club’s first president, the great G.K. Chesterton, died four years later; and every member voted for Bentley to succeed him. Though during that same year ’36 the creator of Philip Trent wrote *Trent’s Own Case* in collaboration with H. Warner Allen, and afterwards provided the *Strand Magazine* with a short-story series collected as *Trent Intervenes*, at the time he was elected club president by acclamation he could show only the solitary, blazing beacon of 1913.

Edmund Clerihew Bentley (1875-1956) had many talents. We who knew him remember him well: burly, quiet-voiced, always with a twinkle in his eye. He wrote *Greedy Night*, that burlesque of a Lord Peter Wimsey adventure which remains at once the funniest and most ingenious parody extant, being both parody and capsule detective story with all the clues. He also invented the casual, adroit verse-form called a clerihew. Thus:

> “Mr. Cecil B. De Mille,  
> Sorely against his will,  
> Was persuaded to leave Moses  
> Out of the Wars of the Roses.”

At the club’s annual dinner thirty-two years ago, held at Grosvenor House, Park Lane, in May of 1938, the principal speaker was an eminent barrister, Norman Birkett, in later years raised to the peerage as Lord Birkett, but even then hero of so many courtroom triumphs, especially as counsel for the defense. Bentley introduced him with a characteristic flourish.

> “Mr. Norman Birkett  
> Travelled the Midland Circuit;  
> He always got the best briefs  
> For defending thiefs.”

Whereupon distinguished counsel arose to demonstrate that this was sober truth, citing his frequent and usually successful efforts to keep out of clink a little pickpocket named Joe. Bentley capped it with a very funny account of the occasion when the British Ambassador to Germany, attending a reception at the home of Field Marshal Goering, had his watch stolen by the Minister of Propaganda, Dr. Josef Goebbels.

It is appropriate that this month's presiding genius should be Bentley's. For once I can offer you four true detective novels, each admirably written and with evidence in full sight. The first and last come from America, the middle two from Britain. Three are new; the fourth, first published little more than a year ago, has just appeared in paperback.

With **The Last Woman in His Life**, by Ellery Queen (World, $5.95) both Ellery the author and Ellery the detective return to the sort of investigation they for so long have strenuously pursued. Outside Wrightsville, that New England witches' cauldron, is one estate of International Playboy Johnny Benedict, who supports three ex-wives and has his eye on a prospective fourth. All three ex-wives are present, together with his tough-minded lawyer and the latter's secretary, when Johnny B. is struck down amid a profusion of clues which may indicate one of those wives or the whole trio together.

We are not far from desperation. A new will, sometimes less a will than a will-o'-the-wisp, floats in the background. The solution is very tricky, and should fool you completely; at least, it fooled me. You may not like this book as well as you liked, say, *Calamity Town* or *Ten Days' Wonder*. And yet any connoisseur can do nothing but admire.

Bentley would have enjoyed the humor and charm as well as the ingenuity of **Many Deadly Returns**, by Patricia Moyes (Holt, Rinehart & Winston, $4.50). Crystal, Lady Balaclava, was once a leader of those Bright Young People who caused their elders so much misgiving in the dizzy nineteen twenties. Time has not dimmed the lady's spirit. Widowed, still wealthy despite taxation, she lives on in that white monstrosity of a country house, with all her bad manners intact.

To celebrate her seventieth birthday, Lady Balaclava's three handsome daughters—one married to a Swiss doctor, the second to a Dutch rose-grower, the third to an American millionaire—arrive from abroad with their customary gifts. Because she fears some hovering menace undefined, old friends of hers in high places send an old friend of ours now Detective Superintendent Henry Tibbett, who with his common-sense wife will keep an eye on the ceremony.

We must all watch closely. The birthday party and tea party becomes a Mad Tea Party with fatal overtones merging into uproar. Can any victim be poisoned when she has ingested no toxic substance? Don't fear cheating; Miss Moyes is too sound a workman to fall back on 'mysterious poisons unknown to science.' There will be a way as well as clues to that way, if we are alert and on guard. And the author has written another first-class story, which discerning readers must not miss.

Though not without faults, Margaret Erskine's **The Case of Mary Fielding** (Crime Club, $4.50) employs straight detection and has strong emotional impact. The presence of beautiful Mary Fielding, battered to death four years before our story opens, can still be felt in the Sussex village where she lived amid friends and relatives as suspicious-looking as picturesque.

Whether or not Gerald Holt communicated with her on some psychic wave-length, the séance that afternoon forms only a prelude to other deeds of blood until Chief Inspector Finch reads their riddle and expounds the truth. Distinctly worth-while.

Though they failed to send me the book in hardcover, this edition of **Sunday the Rabbi Stayed Home**, by Harry Kemelman (Fawcett, 75¢) should please everybody as the third case for that likable cleric, Rabbi David Small of Barnard's Crossing, Mass., who encounters dirty work on his doorstep as inevitably as though he were an urban lieutenant of homicide.

Once more, from the beginning, civil war threatens among his parishioners. Though I can claim little knowledge of Judaism, Rabbi Small's flock sounds very much like the congregation of the Presbyterian Church in which I was brought up. They bicker, they argue, they come almost to blows before natural simmering-down restores their balance. Here are credible human beings entangled in a web of marijuana and murder, with Rabbi Small drawing triumphant conclusions from evidence we should have seen.
