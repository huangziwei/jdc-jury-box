## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

A change in the format of this magazine will mean less space in which the skipper of your friendly column may pontificate. Henceforward, instead of the three pages allowed me for almost a year, we shall revert to the chaste two pages of yore.

Yet to be so restricted can offer positive advantages. There will be more space for appraising the books themselves, and such enforced conciseness should effectually curb your mentor’s congenital fondness for talking too much.

We begin, at least, with a good omen. This month, thank fate, I can offer you four titles, all new, all as expertly contrived as smoothly written: three classic detective novels and one strong mystery-adventure with detective overtones too. Equally divided between British and American, they have been written by a trio of homicidal ladies and one murderous-minded gentleman at the top of his form.

With **A Guilty Thing Surprised**, by Ruth Rendell (Crime Club, $4.50), Mrs. Rendell more than lives up to the reputation for ingenuity and craft she has established in seven earlier novels.

Despite the outwardly placid life of Elizabeth and Quentin Nightingale at Myfield Manor during that golden country weather, decorative Elizabeth goes once too often into the darkling woods near the house, and for no apparent reason is brutally slain. It upsets the husband with whom she gets on almost too well; it even upsets the brother with whom she can’t get on at all, and stills the incessant giggling of an *au pair* girl from Holland. Chief Inspector Wexford, that able investigator we have met before, guides his less sensitive colleague, Inspector Burden, amid evidence as twisted as the emotions behind each move.

English poets and poetry are of importance here. If the main poet had been somebody other than Wordsworth, perhaps Wexford might sooner have realized where to look. You, sir or madam, will be a shrewd old hand if *you* anticipate the end.

**Pick Up Sticks**, by Emma Lathen (Simon and Schuster, $4.95), finds Banker-Detective John Putnam Thatcher away from Wall Street, away from New York and on the Appalachian Trail through New Hampshire with a messily killed corpse in the housing development called Fiord Haven. Distracted as much by high-pressure salesmanship as by the mystery, Thatcher slogs through a profoundly human story to its triumphant end. And, though you must be financially as well as intellectually alert to pick them up, all the clues are there.

If Catherine Aird's **A Late Phoenix** (Crime Club, $4.50) seems as unconventional in its approach as in its construction, this casual air only emphasizes its ultimate dramatic effect.

Set in an English provincial city, investigated by Detective-Inspector Sloan, our problem concerns the skeleton of a once young (and pregnant) girl found under bomb-site rubble long after the end of Hitler's War. Humor, thrill, deft characterization all animate lost hopes, lost fears from a time long gone, when the blood ran fast and these middle-aged people still were young.

**The Green Hell Treasure**, by Robert L. Fish (Putnam, $4.95), proves once more that at sheer storytelling Mr. Fish has no superior in the business. Captain José Da Silva, Interpol and the Brazilian Police, together with his friend Wilson from the American Embassy and alluring Diana Cogswell of Interpol, faces the aftermath of a fifteen-year-old crime as deadly as ever.

The vicious, intelligent leader of four black conspirators from Barbados has just been let out of jail—free, unless Da Silva can nail him, to pick up a fortune in still-hidden loot. Then it's detection as well as action all the way, with surprises at every turn and a final cumulative surprise to top them all. Give it A-plus; it's a beautiful job.
