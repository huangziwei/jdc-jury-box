THE JURY BOX
by JOHN DICKSON CARR

There’s rather an unusual lineup this month. But, though we shall consider only three volumes, one a two-book anthology, the other a shorter omnibus, these three volumes comprise no less than seventeen novels, quite enough weight for any single assessment.

First, demanding attention, come fourteen classics from as many eminent mystery mongers: six British, seven American, one French. No price being quoted, presumably this is a gift edition. The other item on our list provides a trilogy by the same author; fittingly so, since this eminent practitioner has been omitted from the first collection.

Great Stories of Mystery and Suspense, selected and condensed by the editors of the Reader’s Digest (2 vols., Reader’s Digest Association) has both high quality and immense scope.

Though high quality may not always attend joint editorship, where tastes so often clash, there should be little argument about most titles included here. I myself have reservations concerning the hard-boiled, but so many able critics oppose me that I shall not dispute the matter in public.

As for the novels’ variety, themes range from straight detection to intuitive analysis; from Gothic frisson to the convolutions of espionage; from psychological study to slam-bang thrill. Well and sympathetically abridged, they come very near the impossible task of pleasing everybody.

You will find old favorites, as I did. Let me recommend Rex Stout’s The League of Frightened Men, in which Nero Wolfe makes shrewd comments on the nature of violence as he solves his most fascinating problem. With The Death of Don Juan, by Ellery Queen, our own author-detective unravels theatrical tangles from a dying message all but the shrewdest may misinterpret. And what of those marks on the haft of the knife?
