## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

For all your mentor's disbelief in the element called luck, he must admit it's been a lucky season. Though we are almost halfway through the year, worthy items continue to arrive; and, despite my cynicism, I am happy to be proved wrong.

This month you are recommended to three near-masterpieces of international intrigue, each with a flavor of espionage, and the novelized account of a famous Scottish housebreaker, long unsuspected, who robbed neighbors under a most respectable mask in rowdy Edinburgh of the late eighteenth century. Specifically—

**The Left-handed Sleeper** by Ted Willis (Putnam, $7.95) opens with a jolt when Mark Ritchie, respected Member of Parliament as well as prominent personality, suddenly deserts his beautiful wife and disappears. He has bolted just ahead of British intelligence, which holds proof that Ritchie is in fact a secret Russian agent, left "sleeping" for years while he attained his trusted post.

Then begins a polite if uneasy duel between Christine Ritchie, a wife forced to acknowledge her husband's defection but still remaining faithful, and Patrick Cadwall of MI 5, who must track down the traitor but can't help sympathizing with Christine.

When Mark Ritchie is seen in Beirut, Lebanon, the pursuit at last has some clue. They must do more than nail Ritchie himself; they must identify other members of a spy ring in Britain.

Aided by some mysterious source, Christine flies out to join her husband. Pat Cadwall pursues both.

Mayhem and murder erupt when Cadwall’s trail crosses that of certain Soviet agents. As twist succeeds twist, with several more shocks for Christine Ritchie, that same trail leads back to England. There, when the heads of the spy ring are snared through Christine’s own plan, the speeding story sweeps round its final curve to an emotionally as well as politically satisfying end.

Alexandre Dumas’s Monte Cristo was no more intent to destroy an enemy than is Richard Silva, single-minded protagonist of Noel Hynd’s **Revenge** (Dial Press, $7.95).

As an American Air Force officer, captured by North Vietnamese and inhumanly treated by some sadistic but unknown Communist torturer, Silva on his release vows he will find and kill that psycho despite any obstacle.

And the obstacles to finding him are many, since this involves politics; and you mustn’t cause embarrassment to the powers that be. Though many characters of many backgrounds meet or collide during Richard Silva’s saga, there’s no muddle; each has his own place in a pattern of much narrative skill.

Ignoring slights or evasions, Silva stays implacably on course from his first Washington interview to the deadly moment when, after one penultimate surprise, he corners his quarry in a Paris cellar. Always grim, with few concessions to romanticism, **Revenge** is none the less a man-trap that grips and won’t let go.

Some readers may be inclined to regard **The Chinese Fire Drill** by Michael Wolfe (Harper & Row, $7.95) as the most exciting adventure thriller in months. And it would be difficult to argue with this view.

Drawn into the undercover game by Margaret Eriksen, a fetching WAC colonel, Signal Corps Captain Michael Keefe takes cover for what might be called strongarm diplomacy. Evidence shows that Captain Bostwick, son-in-law of influential Senator Colville, is being held to ransom in Vietnam after his capture during a skirmish. At all odds Keefe must rescue Bostwick and his accompanying sergeant if they are still alive.

It’s a task even more formidable than it seems. The title, symbolic rather than descriptive, is explained as rip-roaring action carries our protagonist through every jungle peril and out again in triumph.

Very little fiction has been needed for Forbes Bramble’s **The Strange Case of Deacon Brodie** (Coward, McCann, Geoghegan, $8.95).

Scottish Church deacon, master craftsman, of genteel family and education, by day William Brodie looked as sternly respectable as any other Edinburgh councilman in the year 1788. By night he was addicted to gambling, women, and the bottle; but not so addicted as to interfere with his career as a burglar plundering at will, who had a high old time until his foot slipped.

But did this picturesque soul really die on the new gallows he himself had suggested? The author leaves this question in doubt; and, since Deacon Brodie was never vicious, some of us may hope he escaped the law after all.
