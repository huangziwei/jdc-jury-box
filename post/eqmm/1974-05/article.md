## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Now who shall argue with the luck of the draw? For the second time this year, having received no worthy new fiction in a whole month, I must glance back to reassess the work of some classic mystery-monger from the past. It has been made easy by the able new biography of a more than suitable candidate, which I take as my text for this sermon.

**Melville Davisson Post: Man of Many Mysteries**, by Charles A. Norton (Bowling Green University Popular Press, $8.95 cloth, $3.95 paper), celebrates the multi-talented West Virginian—lawyer, sportsman, criminologist—who ranked among our most successful magazine writers in the blazing heyday of magazines, and even now remains American master of the detective short story.

Born of well-to-do parents in the aristocratic tradition, wide-ranging cosmopolite despite persistent ill health, he was as much at home in England or France as on his own native heath. Yet early influences held fast. Though he created Jonquelle of Paris and Scotland Yard’s Sir Henry Marquis, his best-known tales feature Uncle Abner, stern Calvinist of pre-Civil War hills which had not yet become West Virginia.

He soon became a conscious stylist. His spare, sinewy prose, shorn of excess words but never shorn of vividness or power, anticipated modern trends and set a precedent sometimes forgotten or overlooked by critics today.

The biographer stresses this academic neglect. In his relatively short lifetime (1869-1930) Post handled many themes: the cattlemen’s novel called *Dwellers in the Hills*, the religious allegory of *The Mountain School-Teacher*, factual crime investigation with *The Man Hunters*. But, since most of those who remember him will remember some brilliant short story with a whiplash surprise ending, his biographer resents the critical condescension which can find no depth or profundity here.

Mr. Norton need scarcely have worried. At his best in recreating Melville Post the man and personality, as a good biographer should be, he is perhaps less happy in judging detective fiction, which he does not seem really to understand.

And yet he is right in his complaint. “Depth” and “profundity” are relative terms, too often used for the merely pretentious or phony. Since giants like Mark Twain and G. K. Chesterton always revelled in blood and thunder, any condescending attitude belongs with the snows of yesteryear. A good story needs no justification; let’s make our own appraisal.

Post’s first tales, appearing at the turn of the century, concern the schemes of Randolph Mason, cynical New York attorney, whose clients’ crimes are committed within the framework of the law. The last collected volume, issued after his death, gives us doughty Colonel Braxton, defense counsel of the old tradition and breed.

They were spiritual extremes as well. Through the intervening years this conscientious craftsman perfected his diamond-sharp prose, his atmospheric sense, and the technique of construction by building up mystery and solution together until their ultimate explosion.

For our brief glance, at least, one book should remain without comment. The classic tales in *Uncle Abner, Master of Mysteries* (1918) need no further praise.

As a personal choice, if I may, let me recommend “The Great Cipher,” initial adventure from *Monsieur Jonquelle, Prefect of Police of Paris* (1923). “The Great Cipher,” which will conjure up nightmare images, misleads readers *because* each statement in the evidence is literal truth. It was the favorite Post story of S. S. Van Dine, with whose verdict mature judgment must agree.

There is much to be said for “The Cambered Foot,” from *The Sleuth of St. James’s Square* (1920). And also, in *The Mystery at the Blue Villa* (1919), for the story with that same title. “The Garden in Asia,” from *The Bradmoor Murder* (1929), shows how a railway engine vanished into thin air.

If on some dusty second-hand shelf you find any of these four out-of-print volumes, you will unearth much pleasure. Melville Davisson Post wrote a number of weak stories, as every great craftsman has done. He did not always play fair with the clues. But he was a literary hypnotist *par excellence*, and his sensational effects have never been surpassed.
