## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Optimism can never be cured; human nature forbids. Following last month's famine, which compelled your mentor to fall back on the storage-bin, I had hoped that this time round would see rich crops for harvest. Well, again we avoid extremes. If there has been no overwhelming richness, at least luck has provided four new items which can be recommended without embarrassment.

Since customarily we begin with detection, pride of place must go to **Slayride**, by Dick Francis (Harper & Row, $5.95). Narrator David Cleveland, in Norway as consultant investigator when the disappearance of a British steeplechase-jockey from Oslo's racecourse coincides with the disappearance of sixteen thousand kroner from the till, finds danger-fanged problems on every side. From the moment he is almost run down by a speedboat in icy water, among trainers, owners, and other enthusiasts David reels along slippery ways until the clue in the racing helmet reveals a plot even deeper than he has suspected.

In this firework display perhaps our author holds on a little too long, sending up a solitary Roman candle or two after he has touched off his grand finale set-piece. But reliable Mr. Francis, whose horse sagas contain no horseplay or any less mentionable product, combines all his ingenuity and all his skill at vivid characterization for as enjoyable a performance as readers can expect this year.

You are hereby pleasantly warned that Peter Driscoll's **In Connection with Kilshaw** (Lippincott, $7.95), superior mystery-adventure dipped in espionage, is loaded with high explosive from the start.

Into embattled Belfast, wrecked by the bitter civil strife of northern Ireland, slips a half-Irish Englishman on secret service for Britain. Harry Finn, self-styled newspaperman, must prevent full-scale war by silencing fiery J. C. Kilshaw, leader of the Protestant Loyalist Vigilantes. But equally dangerous loom up opposing Catholic Volunteers under IRA's redoubtable Sullivan, with Finn caught in the middle between both.

Each side is shrewd, crafty, ruthless; each side has its own mysterious plan. To avert some final explosion, without any idea what form that explosion may take, Finn enlists Caragh Hughes, the beautiful red-haired girl who calls herself his enemy. No novel has more vividly pictured envenomed hatred in The Troubles as amid flying bullets this narrative thunders towards its hair-raising climax and its satisfactory end. In mystery-adventure-espionage it may go to the head of the class.

If **The Life and Death of Peter Wade**, by Lionel Black (Stein and Day, $5.95), gives us mystery-adventure-espionage of a more casual or unbuttoned sort, for excitement and trickery it can hold its own amid any such company today.

Easy-going, hard-drinking London Newsman Johnny Trott, through his ex-wife commissioner to write the biography of a late film star suddenly dead, fears it may be pornography he must refuse. But it's not pornographic; it's merely perilous.

Beginning investigation with Norah, one of the film star's most fetching charmers, our protagonist meets uproar from an African republic undermined with secret tunnels of intrigue. Reporter Johnny, so humanly engaging he will capture your sympathy or even your affection, is now after his greatest news story. With London itself a shooting gallery, the chase leads to gunplay in a more-than-sinister embassy, pursuit across roof-tops, and a race to meet his deadline at the office.

Johnny wins; or does he? What has begun in sex, continuing in mystery or violence, now ends in wryness and poetic injustice as a fitting kind of commentary. First class.

Since espionage or counter-espionage in Britain is the whole theme of Owen Sela's **The Kiriov Tapes** (Pantheon, $5.95), brace yourself against wily professionals skilled at the double, the triple, even the quadruple cross.

Despite the presence of an old friend named Maasten, action centers round Adrian Quimper, former police officer now security chief for the most hush-hush bureau. Anatol Kiriov, Soviet defector, demands thirty thousand pounds for disclosures on tape. Kiriov and a reliable British agent are both shot dead in a flat sacred to the agent's mistress; there's treachery as well as security behind the scenes. After some confusion it's inevitable that the double cross should lead to a double twist; believe nothing, good friends, until the last word is said.
