## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

Whatever may be my complaints about items arriving for review, our new books seldom lack variety. In January, to celebrate the new year, I can offer you this choice mixed bag: each hardcover, each hypnotic, ranging from a three-volume single-author omnibus to a really memorable crime anthology, with stops between them for one novel of detection and one of espionage.

**The Charlotte Armstrong Festival**, by that late brilliant lady (Coward, McCann & Geoghegan, $8.95), comprises three romances in which Miss Armstrong has so well shown her dexterity at what might be called the emotional thriller.

Whether you begin with “The Gift Shop,” “Lemon in the Basket,” or “The Balloon Man,” you will find her sympathetic protagonist enmeshed in mystery or dilemma which grows ever more tense until our hostess restores clarity with some ingenious touch at the finale.

If feminine coyness sometimes intrudes, it never diminishes humor, perception, or impact. My own favorite would be “The Gift Shop,” wherein Harry Fairchild and his faithful Jean search abroad as well as at home for the secret of a toy pig at Los Angeles Airport. But I should not contest some other view. As connoisseurs, *mes enfants*, you will want to try them all.

With Jack Fenner, Boston private investigator of George Harmon Coxe’s **No Place for Murder** (Knopf, $6.95), we plunge at once into more fictional dirty work than that sedate town has known since Jacques Futrelle created the Thinking Machine.

First Fenner must prove he didn’t kill the unknown man found shot in his office. By tracking that victim, dubious both past and present, they uncover evidence of large-scale jugglery in stock certificates. This may or may not involve Bruce Townsend, Brahmin stockbroker, who has lost his head over a would-be actress.

Lieutenant Bacon, though less cooperative than some policemen, at least won’t impede Fenner as our hero scurries to investigate.

It’s a broad field. Honest or otherwise, Bruce Townsend owes Maurie Matlock thirty thousand from one ill-judged bet; Maurie’s hoods are closing in. Do you understand the meaning of “vigorish”?

Despite so many suspects, Fenner hits on truth through various clues and traps the murderer before another bullet can be fired, thus fittingly to conclude the latest detective story from an old pro whose hand has not lost its cunning in forty years.

The first assignment for James Hagen, alert CIA trainee who narrates **Pay on the Way Out**, by John Murphy (Scribners, $6.95), is to make a field evaluation of three widely separated murders—the Spanish toreador shot during a bullfight, the London journalist knifed in his mews cottage, the CIA official killed by explosive at Arlington, Virginia—when there may well be some undercover link between them.

This link between them proves more than casual. Hagen, whose fluent Spanish eases his task, begins to interpret the connection from certain photographs taken during the famous San Firmin fiesta at Pamplona.

Though we meet the most modern espionage, involving France as well as Russia, beneath outward chicanery seethes another lethal intrigue. Its trail carries Hagen from Spain to England and then back to Spain for a showdown.

Showdown with death? Menaced by more than one secret enemy, he escapes through the intervention of Marian Chelt, the English girl who has followed him. If at first glance the enemy seems to win and wax fat, it’s sheer illusion. The author feels as sympathetic towards Jim Hagen as you yourself will feel; be sure that villainy gets its comeuppance, and bad guys pay on the way out.

**Open at Your Own Risk**, edited by Joan Kahn (Houghton Mifflin, $12.95), provides a beautifully mixed bag of chills or thrills, fiction and fact, for her seventh distinguished anthology.

With unerring eye Miss Kahn has sought more obscure material in search of novelty. Many titles will be unfamiliar; even your obedient servant failed to identify a few. Though W. F. Harvey’s “The Beast with Five Fingers” has long been my favorite ghost story, and the trial of Elvira Barney made criminal history at the Old Bailey, I never encountered any experiment in grisliness by W. W. Jacobs except “The Monkey’s Paw.”

Apart from a Wodehouse caper, doubtless included for contrast, there’s crime or diabolism to suit every taste. Dip in anywhere, but expect only the unexpected, which after all is an even more pleasant form of risk.
