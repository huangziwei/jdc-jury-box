## The Jury Box

by John Dickson Carr

So long as we still have at least half a dozen mystery mongers who combine ingenuity with fair play, the noble craft of the detective novel is in no danger. Otherwise their methods vary, as indeed methods should. And the writers of espionage thrillers, though bound by few rules, may well adopt some part of the same technique.

Four new items this month, two detective and two espionage, seem to bear out the not-very-profound remarks above. Let me try to demonstrate my point.

In Emma Lathen’s **By Hook or By Crook** (Simon and Schuster, $6.95), an expertly planned crime once more forces the dignified old Sloan Guaranty Trust into indignities from which it must be rescued by John Putnam Thatcher, its senior vice-president.

At once we become embroiled in the troubles of Parajians, Inc., those famous (and dignified) importers of Oriental rugs, whose founder, Paul Parajian, rules his relentlessly squabbling family. All our Sloan friends are within call—Everett Gabler, Charlie Trinkham, ultra-correct Miss Corsa—when a woman claiming to be Paul Parajian’s sister is poisoned soon after she has landed in New York.

But that’s only for openers. With kindly satire, with a keen eye for irony, the author studies emotional pressures through another murder, through the great auction at Parajian’s, until Thatcher gathers up every clue in as fine a detective story as Emma Lathen has written.

Recalling events all too real, **Time of Terror**, by Hugh Pentecost (Dodd, Mead, $5.95), postulates a suite in Manhattan’s luxury Hotel Beaumont taken over by murderous hoods calling themselves the Army for Justice under their self-styled Colonel Coriander.

It’s the suite of Terrence Cleaves, British Ambassador to the United Nations. Heavily armed, with enough explosives to wreck the whole hotel, these terrorists hold Ambassador Cleaves’s two small daughters, who will suffer mutilation unless the American government meets certain outrageous political demands, of which the fifty-million-dollar ransom is only a small part.

“Colonel Coriander,” identity hidden by mask and wig, has a spy to keep him informed. As intimidation becomes outright murder, Hotel Manager Pierre Chambrun seems defeated. Mr. Pentecost, varying the approach, is concerned less with satire than with increasing tension until it explodes. Though he gives you all the evidence, you should find more than one surprise as Chambrun traps the mastermind for the comeuppance he deserves.

In Michael Bar-Zohar’s **The Spy Who Died Twice** (Houghton Mifflin, $6.95) the young Israeli author of *The Third Eye* again triumphs with a second espionage adventure featuring Jeff Saunders, C.I.A.

It is established that Denis Egleton, British agent who also spied for Russia, died and was buried in England. A year later, however, somebody kills the same man during Voodoo ceremonies in Haiti. This flat impossibility, in some way associated with the shooting of a Greek tycoon on his yacht as well as with a treason trial at the Old Bailey, sends Jeff Saunders on a danger-fanged whirl across half the earth. Even when one statesman has been cleared of the charge against him, still more problems remain.

The author, adopting detective-story technique, uncorks a twist ending which has been carefully prepared. The plot is ingenious, the pace fast, the characterization vivid. Here’s cloak-and-dagger at its best.

**The Judas Sheep** by Jan Roberts (Dutton, $6.95) centers round Richard Burns, London art dealer and amateur secret agent, as the mission he has accepted under protest carries him from New York to so many perilous corners of Greece.

Certain C.I.A. bigwigs, sneakier than those in the preceding novel, send this adventurous Briton to learn whether his old friend Stavros Alexandratos, Greek painter and former underground leader, may still be alive despite presumed death in a car smash.

But it’s all peril. Flung headlong into a saga of murder, treachery, and buried gold, not forgetting his romance with the beautiful Eleni; Burns remains level-headed enough to out-maneuver menaces even more unpleasant than overfat Kostandis or masquerading Captain Dimitriou. A smashing climax at the monastery leads to ironic farewells before Burns and Eleni board the plane that will carry them to safety. Another for your list.
