## Best Mysteries of the Month

recommended by John Dickson Carr

The new year, let it be reported, already glows with promise. At least (praise be!) we have had no campaign against violence in published mysteries. On this occasion I can offer you three explosive novels and a book of admirable short stories. Should 1970 continue as it has begun, there will be scant need to fall back on Father Brown, Joseph Rouletabille, or Uncle Abner.

**The Brass Go-Between**, by Oliver Bleeck (Morrow, $5.95) packs T.N.T. in both fists; don't underrate its author, as a mere Fancy Dan. After some dazzling footwork in early rounds, feinting as well as jabbing with his left, Mr. Bleeck—who's really somebody else—cuts loose with a haymaking right-cross that may dump you on the canvas, and a double-cross even after the bell.

Less figuratively, it concerns Philip St. Ives, professional negotiator between robbed and robbers for the return of heisted loot, when the ancient brass shield of Komporeen, sacred symbol to both warring factions of a new African republic, is stolen from the Coulter Museum in Washington. The museum's billionaire patron will pay almost any ransom for the shield. But St. Ives, as go-between, must first find the thieves and negotiate.

Beginning at the office of enigmatic Frances Wingo, he shuttles between Washington and New York in the heat of August no less than the heat of a death-pursuit that leaves corpses behind; the cops can't be blamed for their lack of sympathy. Everybody wants that shield, from American hoods to giant, amiable, Sandhurst-trained Colonel Mbwato of Jandola, on the side of the government in the Jandolan civil war.

St. Ives's chase roars to its finale in the Virginia countryside, reaching crescendo at the scene of some famous violence in another civil war: the battlefield of Manassas. The author plays fair throughout; and, if he is almost the only one in the story who does, that's the plan or purpose of all good mysteries.
